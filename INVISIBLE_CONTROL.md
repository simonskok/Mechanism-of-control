# Invisible Forms of Government and Control

**A structural reference on how power sustains itself without appearing as power.**

Merged master document. Combines the core thinker and domain research with the milieu-based
research extension. Source content for a public web project aimed at readers roughly 18 to 40.

Version 2.0, September 2026.

---

## How this document is organized

Two organizing principles run through it, and both are deliberate.

**Part Two is organized by thinker.** Six figures carry the analytic weight, and each one
needs its mechanism laid out step by step, because the mechanism is the actual content.

**Part Four is organized by milieu.** A cluster of thinkers is understood through the
institution that housed them, the money that paid for the work, the crisis they were writing
inside, and the boundary of what could be said there. The reason for that structure is in
Part One, section 0.2. A section that becomes a gallery of great men has failed.

Neither principle is sufficient alone. Foucault needs his argument reconstructed; the Macy
Conferences need their funding traced. Read Part Two for what the arguments say and Part Four
for why those arguments became sayable when they did.

Entries in Part Two follow a fixed structure so the site can render them consistently: thesis
in one sentence, the mechanism, key concepts, psychological underpinning, primary works,
serious objections, where to go next.

Entries in Part Four follow the extension structure: setting, climate, who was in the room,
key works and the insight, what it adds, institutional history.

Nothing here should be published as written. It is dense, it assumes context, and it uses
terms that must be defined on first contact for a general reader.

**House style, applied throughout and required of anything added later.** Plain hyphens, no
em dashes. No shipping or maritime metaphors. Warm plain language. No filler. Say UNKNOWN
rather than filling a gap, and never invent a date, an institution, a funder or a quotation.

# PART ONE: METHOD

## 0.1 The single insight underneath all of it

Six major thinkers, working in different countries, different decades, and from openly
hostile political positions, converge on one structural observation:

> **Power is most effective where it is least visible as power.**

Domination that must be enforced by visible force is weak, expensive, and unstable.
Domination that has been converted into common sense, merit, normality, law, care, or
personal identity is cheap, stable, and self-repairing, because the people subject to it
carry it out themselves and experience it as freedom, fairness, or simply reality.

The six angles on the same move:

| Thinker | Institution examined | The mask it wears | What is actually happening |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bourdieu | School | Neutral meritocracy | Inherited class advantage laundered into certified merit |
| Gramsci | Culture, media, church, unions | Common sense | A ruling class's particular interest presented as the universal interest |
| Schmitt | Legal order | Impartial rule of law | A pre-legal decision by a concrete authority sits at the foundation |
| Foucault | School, clinic, prison, workplace | Humane progress, expertise | A technology producing docile bodies, norms, and a self that polices itself |
| Illich | School, medicine, transport, professions | Care and service | Manufactured dependence, and institutions producing the opposite of their promise |
| Agamben | Security state | Temporary emergency | Exception made permanent, politics administering bare biological life |

Three of these thinkers are of the left, one (Schmitt) of the authoritarian right, and the
others resist the axis entirely. **That is the strongest evidence that the insight is
structural rather than partisan.** This must be foregrounded on the site. It is the difference
between an analytic project and a political recruitment vehicle.

## 0.2 Read the room, not the man

A recurring failure in this literature is treating ideas as biography. It produces a gallery
of thinkers and explains nothing about why a given idea became sayable at a given moment, or
who paid for the thinking.

Four questions to put to every source in this document:

1. **Who paid, and what did they want?** Foundation, military, party, industry, university.
   Follow the grant, not the footnote. Almost every mid-century communications and behavioural
   finding traces to a military or foundation programme with an applied goal.
2. **What was the institution's actual job?** A bureau measuring radio audiences for
   advertisers produces a different theory of the public than a seminar in exile writing
   against fascism, even when the two share a building.
3. **What crisis was in the room?** Total war, occupation, decolonization, inflation, a
   collapsing empire. Ideas about control get written when control is visibly failing or
   visibly being built.
4. **What could not be said there?** The boundary of the sayable inside an institution is
   often the most informative thing about it.

Read institutional histories alongside primary texts. Where a good institutional history
exists, it is named in the cluster.

Follow the money as a matter of routine, not as an accusation. Funding shapes findings through
selection and incentive far more often than through instruction, and the selection story is
both truer and more interesting than the instruction story.

## 0.3 The standard of rigor

This body of thought has two failure modes, and everything built from it must resist both.

**Failure mode A, the sanctioned critique.** Organizations funded by, and dependent on access
to, the institutions they study produce criticism that never threatens the source of funding.
The result is alternatives that reinforce the original arrangement. The leash is not
censorship, it is incentive. Any organization living on state or supranational grants and on
official access is structurally selected for this. Part Four, section 4.4 on the Congress for
Cultural Freedom is the historical proof of concept.

**Failure mode B, the conspiratorial monism.** The mirror image. Because these frameworks
explain so much, they tempt the user into explaining everything: monocausal stories,
unfalsifiable grand narratives, a self-flattering sense of having seen through it all. This is
not strength. It is the same laziness wearing black.

**The distinguishing criterion is falsifiability and evidence, not edginess.** Foucault,
Bourdieu, Scott, Mitchell and Graeber have standing because of brutal archival and empirical
work, not because of suspicion.

Editorial rules that follow:

1. Every claim traced to a source that can be checked.
2. Every thinker's section includes the strongest objections to that thinker.
3. Distinguish **structural tendency** (institutions systematically produce outcome X because
   of how incentives are arranged) from **conscious conspiracy** (named people met and
   agreed). The first is usually true and far more interesting. The second is usually false
   and always more tempting. Note the important exception: Part Four, section 4.6 on the
   neoliberal thought collective is a documented case of deliberate long-horizon coordination,
   which is exactly why it needs the highest evidentiary standard rather than the lowest.
4. Distinguish what a source **established** from what it **asserted**. Flag contested and
   discredited work as contested, and say who contests it. Wittfogel, MacLean, Le Bon, Stonor
   Saunders, Milgram and Zimbardo are all in this document because they need that handling.
5. Where a framework fails a case, say so. Agamben on COVID (section 2.6) is the worked
   example and should be published as such.
6. Where a cluster is North Atlantic by default, say so, and name what the colonial or
   peripheral version would look like.
7. Do not flatten the politics. This literature runs across the spectrum and several figures
   are politically ugly. Engage the argument rather than avoiding or endorsing it.

**Terminology note.** The phrase "dark psychology" is retired throughout. It is a
pop-self-help label with no scholarly content and it costs credibility with exactly the
readers worth having. The rigorous material is far more disturbing than the phrase suggests.
See Part Three, section 3.4.

# PART TWO: THE SIX LOAD-BEARING THINKERS

## 2.1 Pierre Bourdieu (1930 to 2002, France)

**Milieu.** Algeria during the war of independence, where his fieldwork began, then the École
Normale generation and the Collège de France. The crisis in the room was the postwar
expansion of French higher education, which was supposed to open the elite and demonstrably
did not.

**Thesis.** The school presents itself as the neutral instrument that rewards talent, while in
fact rewarding cultural advantages transmitted at home, thereby converting inherited privilege
into certified merit and getting the losers to accept the verdict as a truth about their own
nature.

**The mechanism, step by step:**

1. A child absorbs, from family and environment, a **habitus**: durable dispositions below
   conscious choice, including accent, bearing, taste, and ease or unease with abstraction and
   authority. Bourdieu calls it structure made flesh. Critically, it feels natural rather than
   inherited.
2. Some habitus carry **cultural capital**: the legitimate vocabulary, familiarity with valued
   culture, comfort in institutional settings. It exists in three states. *Embodied* (in the
   person: manner, accent, confidence), *objectified* (books, instruments, art at home), and
   *institutionalized* (diplomas, credentials).
3. The school enforces a standard it presents as universal but which is in fact the **cultural
   arbitrary**: the specific culture of the dominant class, misrecognized as culture as such.
4. Children arriving with matching cultural capital experience school as continuous with home,
   and learning feels like a gift. Others must consciously and laboriously acquire what the
   first group absorbed for free.
5. The gap appears not as a difference in inherited capital but as a difference in **ability**.
6. The school certifies the outcome with credentials. Class advantage enters, merit exits.
   **The social origin of the advantage is erased in the conversion.**

**Symbolic violence.** Violence because the damage is real: subordination, foreclosed lives.
Symbolic because it works through meaning and categories of perception rather than force. And
it operates **with the complicity of the dominated**. The student who says "I'm just not
academic" has internalized the institution's verdict as a fact about their own nature and
executes the sentence themselves. **Misrecognition** (*méconnaissance*) is the technical term:
the dominated accept the legitimacy of the very standard that ranks them low. The violence
succeeds only because it is not perceived as violence.

**The ideology of giftedness** is the keystone. Attributing success to natural talent rather
than transmitted capital converts a socially produced inequality into an apparently natural
one, and persuades winners and losers alike.

**Why credential meritocracy legitimizes inequality better than aristocracy did.** Open
aristocracy is visibly unjust and therefore contestable. Meritocracy produces winners who
sincerely believe they earned it and losers who believe they deserved it, and the system can
point to a handful of risers as proof that anyone could. See Michael Young's 1958 satire *The
Rise of the Meritocracy*, which coined the word as a warning and was then adopted as a
compliment, itself a perfect case study.

**Psychological underpinning.** Internalization of external evaluation. Self-concept formed by
institutional feedback. Stereotype threat and self-handicapping. The just-world hypothesis
(Melvin Lerner): people need to believe outcomes are deserved, so they rationalize their own
subordination.

**Primary works.** *Reproduction in Education, Society and Culture* (with Jean-Claude Passeron,
1970). *The Inheritors* (1964). *Distinction* (1979). *The Forms of Capital* (1986, the single
best short entry point). *Language and Symbolic Power* (1991).

**Serious objections.** Charged with determinism, since habitus appears to leave little room
for agency or change. Empirical strength of cultural-capital effects varies substantially by
country and by which measures are used; effects are typically real but smaller than the
theory's rhetoric implies. The theory explains reproduction better than it explains mobility
or rupture.

**Where to go next.** Basil Bernstein on elaborated versus restricted linguistic codes. Paul
Willis, *Learning to Labour* (1977), an ethnography of working-class boys whose resistance to
school delivers them into working-class jobs, a crucial complication of Bourdieu. Annette
Lareau, *Unequal Childhoods* (2003), the American empirical version, concerted cultivation
versus natural growth.

## 2.2 Antonio Gramsci (1891 to 1937, Italy)

**Milieu, and it matters more than usual.** Wrote the *Prison Notebooks* in Mussolini's
prisons. The prosecutor said the aim was to stop his brain working for twenty years. The text
is fragmentary by necessity and written in code to evade the censor, so "the philosophy of
praxis" means Marxism throughout. This is a case where the institution housing the thinker was
literally a prison, and the boundary of the sayable is visible on every page.

**The problem he was solving.** Orthodox Marxism predicted that capitalism's contradictions
would drive workers to revolt. In the advanced West it did not happen. Workers frequently
supported the order subordinating them, and fascism won mass consent. Why?

**Thesis.** The ruling class does not rule primarily by force. It rules by **consent**, by
making its own particular interests appear to be the universal interest, absorbed as obvious
common sense. Coercion is held in reserve. The daily work is manufacturing agreement.

**Key concepts:**

- **Hegemony.** Leadership by consent, distinct from domination by force. A class is hegemonic
  when its worldview has become the ambient, unquestioned frame.
- **Political society.** The coercive apparatus: government, courts, police, army.
- **Civil society.** Schools, churches, press, publishers, unions, cultural associations,
  everyday intellectual life. **This is where hegemony is manufactured.** The full state is
  the combination, which Gramsci describes as hegemony armoured by coercion.
- **War of maneuver versus war of position.** See Part Five, the East and West axis.
- **Common sense** (*senso comune*). The incoherent inherited worldview of ordinary people:
  folklore, religion, fragments of old philosophy, ruling-class ideas absorbed without
  examination. Hegemony works through common sense, which is its medium. Gramsci contrasts it
  with *good sense*, the kernel of practical critical insight inside it.
- **Traditional intellectuals.** The professor, the cleric. Imagine themselves autonomous and
  above class while servicing the established order.
- **Organic intellectuals.** Arise from a class and give its raw worldview coherence and
  articulacy. Any class seeking hegemony, or seeking to break one, must produce its own.
- **Organic crisis.** Hegemony breaks down and coercion comes into the open. Hence the most
  quoted line: the old is dying, the new cannot be born, and in the interregnum a great
  variety of morbid symptoms appears.

**The payload.** Consent is engineered so that force becomes unnecessary. Where hegemony is
secure you rarely need police, because people police themselves and each other.

**Psychological underpinning.** System justification theory (John Jost): the empirically
documented tendency of people, including the disadvantaged, to defend and rationalize the
status quo, sometimes more strongly the worse their position. This is the closest modern
experimental analogue to hegemony and should be paired with it on the site.

**Primary works.** *Selections from the Prison Notebooks* (Hoare and Nowell-Smith, 1971) is
the standard English gateway. Secondary: Stuart Hall's essays on Gramsci. Perry Anderson, *The
Antinomies of Antonio Gramsci*, the sharpest internal critique.

**Serious objections.** Hegemony is elastic to the point of unfalsifiability. If consent proves
hegemony and dissent proves incipient counter-hegemony, nothing could refute it. The
fragmentary text permits selective quotation for almost any position, and both left and right
now claim the framework. Anderson demonstrates that Gramsci uses the state and civil society
distinction in at least three incompatible ways.

**Adjacent, and now in this document.** Michels on the iron law of oligarchy (section 4.5)
reaches a structurally similar conclusion from the opposite direction, arguing that
organization itself produces a leadership stratum with distinct interests. Gramsci wanted
counter-hegemonic organization; Michels says organization is where the problem starts. That
tension is unresolved and worth working on.

## 2.3 Carl Schmitt (1888 to 1985, Germany)

**Mandatory framing.** Schmitt joined the Nazi party in 1933 and became the regime's leading
legal apologist, including an essay justifying the extrajudicial killings of the Night of the
Long Knives. He is not a neutral analyst. He is the sharpest diagnostician of liberalism's
foundations because he was its enemy hunting for soft ground. **Take the scalpel, reject the
surgery.** The site must never present him without this.

**Milieu.** Weimar, and the crisis in the room is the one that defines him: a constitutional
republic governing by emergency decree under Article 48, with paramilitaries in the streets
and a parliament unable to form majorities. His theory is a description of what he was
watching, converted into a general claim.

**Thesis.** *Sovereign is he who decides on the exception.* (*Political Theology*, 1922)

**Unpacking it:**

- **The exception** (*Ausnahmezustand*, state of emergency or exception) is the genuine crisis
  the legal order did not and could not foresee. The case the rules do not cover, the
  emergency threatening the order's survival.
- Law is general and written in advance. It cannot specify in advance what to do in the truly
  unforeseen case.
- So someone must decide two things: **that** an exception exists, and **what to do**,
  including suspending normal law to save the order the law exists to protect.
- That someone is the sovereign.

**The relocation.** Sovereignty moves from the centre of the system (who makes ordinary law) to
its border (who can suspend all law when it counts). Sovereignty is a **borderline concept**,
found not in normal functioning but in the limit case. The exception is more interesting than
the rule: in normal times the machinery hums and the sovereign is invisible, and the emergency
pulls the curtain and shows who actually decides.

**Decisionism**, aimed at legal positivism and specifically at Hans Kelsen. The positivists
held law to be a self-sufficient hierarchy of norms grounded in a basic norm, rules all the
way down. Schmitt says that at the bottom sits not a norm but a **decision**: a concrete act
of will by a concrete authority, founding the order and able to suspend it. For rules to apply
at all you first need a pacified, ordered situation in which they can apply, and producing that
normality is itself a sovereign act not governed by the rules it makes possible. So the
foundation of all legality is **pre-legal and a-legal**: a will, not a rule.

**Political theology.** All significant concepts of the modern theory of the state are
secularized theological concepts. The sovereign suspending law in the exception is the
political analogue of the miracle, God suspending the natural laws He authored. The liberal
constitutional state corresponds to the deist god who built the machine and never intervenes.
Schmitt considers this a self-flattering fiction: a state denying it has a sovereign has not
abolished that power, only hidden where it sits, so it will be paralyzed in a real crisis or
captured by whoever is willing to decide.

**Also relevant.** *The Concept of the Political* (1932): the political is defined by the
**friend and enemy distinction**, and every genuinely political relation reduces to the
possibility of grouping people as friends and enemies. Read as diagnosis of how political
mobilization works, and as warning about where it goes.

**The diagnostic to take.** Whenever an institution presents itself as pure neutral procedure,
a government of laws and not of men, ask who decides when the rules get suspended, and who
decided the rules into being.

**Psychological underpinning.** Legitimacy as belief rather than fact (Weber). The human
tendency to treat the existing legal frame as natural and eternal rather than instituted.

**Primary works.** *Political Theology* (1922). *The Concept of the Political* (1932). *The
Crisis of Parliamentary Democracy* (1923).

**Serious objections.** The framework can be used to justify anything a strongman does, and
historically was. His account of liberalism is a caricature built to be knocked over, and it
underrates the actual capacity of constitutional orders to handle emergency through legal
means. The whole construction has an obvious motive: it makes authoritarian decision the truth
of politics and parliamentary deliberation a sham.

**Where to go next.** Kelsen's *Pure Theory of Law*, the position Schmitt attacks; read both.
Agamben (section 2.6). Jan-Werner Müller, *A Dangerous Mind*, on Schmitt's postwar influence
across the spectrum. Bertrand de Jouvenel, *On Power* (1945), section 4.5, for a different
route to a comparable pessimism written under occupation.

## 2.4 Michel Foucault (1926 to 1984, France)

**Milieu.** Postwar French philosophy, the psychiatric hospital as his early empirical site,
then the Collège de France. The crisis in the room by the late 1960s is the discovery that the
humane, reforming, therapeutic institutions of the welfare state were themselves instruments
of control, which is the finding that gives his work its charge.

**First move: demolishing the standard model of power.** Most people carry an unexamined
**juridical-sovereign model**, in which power is something the state possesses, located at the
top, exercised downward, working by prohibition: the law that says no, forbids, censors,
represses. Power as essentially negative. Foucault says this picture is a decoy. In political
theory we still have not cut off the king's head.

**Counter-model: power is relational, capillary, and productive.**

- **Relational.** Not a substance held but something exercised in relations. A verb, not a
  noun. It exists only in being put into action.
- **Capillary.** Not radiating from one centre but circulating through the finest vessels:
  classroom, clinic, workshop, family, how you are taught to sit, speak and attend.
- **Productive.** Power does not mainly repress pre-existing things, it **produces**:
  knowledge, norms, and subjects. The repressive hypothesis gets it backwards. The Victorians
  did not silence sex, they generated a proliferating discourse about it, including
  confessions, classifications, medical categories, and the invention of the homosexual as a
  species of person.

**Power/knowledge** (*pouvoir/savoir*). The slash is the argument. They are not separate things
that meet. No exercise of power without organizing a field of knowledge, and no field of
knowledge without presupposing relations of power. The prison generates criminology, and
criminology underwrites the prison. **This is why neutral expertise warrants suspicion. Not
because experts lie, but because producing the truth about a domain is simultaneously producing
power over it.**

**The historical demonstration, *Discipline and Punish* (1975).** Opens with the public
execution and dismemberment of the regicide Damiens, then contrasts it with a drab prison
timetable eighty years later. The obvious reading is humanitarian progress. Foucault's reading
is a different and far more efficient technology.

- **Sovereign power.** Spectacular, intermittent, aimed at the body in dramatic punishment. It
  writes the law on the criminal's flesh for all to see, but does not reach into daily life.
- **Disciplinary power.** Continuous, invisible, mundane, aimed at producing useful and
  obedient bodies through small constant techniques: the timetable, the drill, ranked rows of
  desks, the partitioning of space, minute regulation of activity. It manufactures **docile
  bodies**, simultaneously productive and politically harmless. Perfected in monasteries,
  armies and workshops, then generalized to school, hospital and factory, until society itself
  is disciplinary.

**The three instruments:**

1. **Hierarchical observation.** Architecture and arrangement that render people permanently
   visible to authority.
2. **Normalizing judgment.** A constant micro-penality of the slightly off (late, inattentive,
   awkward), measured against a **norm**. Discipline does not punish illegality, it corrects
   deviation from a standard of normality it produced itself.
3. **The examination.** Test, inspection, review. Turns each person into a **case**: a
   documented individual with a file, a record, a rankable position in a distribution.
   **The modern individual is therefore not what power suppresses but what disciplinary power
   fabricates.**

**The Panopticon.** Bentham's design: a ring of backlit cells around a central tower, so the
inmate is always potentially watched and never able to verify whether he is watched now.
Uncertainty does the work. The watcher need not be present. The inmate must behave as if
watched at all times, internalizes the gaze, and becomes **the principle of his own
subjection**. Power becomes automatic and de-individualized. This is the mechanical version of
the same move found in Bourdieu and Gramsci: domination at maximum efficiency when the
dominated administer it themselves and experience it as being a normal, functioning person.

**Two later widenings:**

- **Biopower and biopolitics.** Alongside discipline, which targets the individual body,
  emerges power targeting the **population** as biological mass: birth rates, mortality,
  health, hygiene, the life of the species. The sovereign's old right was to take life or let
  live. The biopolitical formula inverts it: **to make live and let die.** This is the door
  Agamben walks through, and Mbembe's necropolitics (section 4.8) is the view of it from the
  colony.
- **Governmentality.** Modern liberal government works less by crushing freedom than by
  shaping it, which he calls **the conduct of conduct**. It structures the field of possible
  actions so that free subjects, pursuing what feel like their own choices, conduct themselves
  in governable ways. You are not forced. You are configured to want what keeps the system
  running. Nikolas Rose (section 4.8) is the most useful extension of this.

**The final payload.** Power produces the **subject** in both senses: subject to authority, and
a subject with an interior identity to know, confess and improve. Your self-knowledge is not
the bedrock beneath power but one of its products.

**Psychological underpinning.** Self-surveillance and internalized evaluation. The confessional
mode as a technique of subject formation. The normalizing power of psychometrics and diagnostic
categories.

**Primary works.** *Discipline and Punish* (1975), start here. *The History of Sexuality Vol.
1* (1976). *Madness and Civilization* (1961). *The Birth of the Clinic* (1963). The lectures
*Security, Territory, Population* and *The Birth of Biopolitics* (1978 to 1979).

**Serious objections.** Historically loose in places, and specialists have contested his account
of the Great Confinement and of pre-modern madness. If power is everywhere and produces the
subject, it is unclear what grounds resistance or on what basis anything is criticized as bad,
a normative gap that Habermas and Nancy Fraser press hard. Charged with functionalism, since
institutions appear too neatly to serve a purpose nobody chose.

**The essential update.** Deleuze's six-page "Postscript on the Societies of Control" (1990),
section 4.8, argues that the enclosures Foucault described were already dissolving as he wrote.
Read it directly after *Discipline and Punish*, not months later.

## 2.5 Ivan Illich (1926 to 2002, Austria and Mexico)

**Milieu, and it explains the work.** A Catholic priest who worked in a Puerto Rican parish in
New York, then as vice-rector of the Catholic University of Puerto Rico, then founded CIDOC in
Cuernavaca, Mexico, in 1961. CIDOC trained missionaries, and Illich used it partly to argue
that the missionary and development apparatus should not exist. He was writing from inside an
institution he was dismantling, funded by language-course fees rather than by foundations,
which is a rare and relevant funding structure. Investigated by the Vatican in 1968.

**Thesis.** Institutions that present themselves as our benefactors, including school,
medicine, transport and the professions, past a threshold of scale produce the opposite of
what they promise, while making us grateful for it and incapable without them.

**Deschooling Society (1971).** School installs a set of confusions we then mistake for
reality: **teaching with learning, grades with education, attendance with knowledge, diplomas
with competence**. We come to believe valuable learning must be produced by a certified
institution, measured and credentialed, when most of what anyone knows was learned outside such
a process. The damage is not in the syllabus but in the **hidden curriculum**, the lesson
taught by school's form regardless of content: that life consists of consuming institutionally
packaged services, that you cannot do important things yourself, that process equals substance.
School's deepest teaching is **dependence on institutions**. It manufactures people who, facing
any need, look for the accredited provider.

**Radical monopoly**, the most exportable concept in this document. An ordinary monopoly is one
brand cornering a market. A radical monopoly is one *mode* of meeting a need so dominating the
field that all other modes become invisible, illegitimate or impossible.

- *The car.* It does not merely outcompete other transport, it remakes the built environment
  around itself, stretching cities, gutting transit, making distances long and streets
  dangerous, until walking and cycling stop being real options. You do not choose a car so much
  as get conscripted into needing one.
- *Schooling.* Once it holds the monopoly, unschooled learning does not count, and the
  autodidact's knowledge is invisible for lack of a credential.

It is radical because it operates at the root, on the need itself, foreclosing alternatives
before they can be conceived. See section 8.3: this is the strongest unused concept for
platform analysis, sharper than most contemporary monopoly language.

**Counterproductivity.** The general law: institutions past a threshold generate the very evil
they were built to cure. Illich describes **two watersheds**, and past the second the tool turns
on its master.

- *Medical Nemesis* (1975) and **iatrogenesis**, sickness caused by medicine. *Clinical* (drug
  effects, hospital infection), *social* (medicalization of ordinary life, expanding definitions
  of illness), and *cultural*, the deepest, where the medical system expropriates our capacity
  to face our own pain, suffering, healing and death, leaving clients who have forgotten how to
  be ill or to die without management.
- Transport past a threshold produces immobility and consumes the time it saves. *Energy and
  Equity* (1974) contains the calculation.
- Schools past a threshold produce ignorance and learned helplessness.
- Communications industries produce silence.

**Disabling professions.** Professions do not merely serve needs, they **define** them,
monopolize the authority to name what you lack and what will fix it, and thereby disable lay
competence. You are converted from a person who does into a client who receives.

**Conviviality (*Tools for Conviviality*, 1973).** A **convivial tool** is one anyone can pick
up, when and as they wish, to realize their own purpose, and it enlarges autonomy and
competence. Examples: the bicycle, the library, the telephone, hand tools. A **manipulative or
industrial tool** enforces dependence, requires a certified priesthood, dictates the user's
behaviour, and reshapes the world so you must come to it. The political project is to keep
tools below the threshold at which they stop serving conviviality.

**The through-line.** Illich's institutions, like Foucault's disciplines and Bourdieu's school,
wear a benevolent mask (we teach you, heal you, move you, care for you) while producing
dependence and the opposite of their promise. The masterstroke is that you experience the
dependence as care and want the service that disables you.

**Why he matters most for building.** Illich is the only figure in Part Two oriented toward
constructing alternatives rather than only diagnosing. Anything built out of this research
should treat *Tools for Conviviality* as its design brief.

**Psychological underpinning.** Learned helplessness (Seligman). Deskilling and the atrophy of
self-efficacy. The way institutional framing colonizes how a need is even experienced.

**Primary works.** *Deschooling Society* (1971). *Tools for Conviviality* (1973). *Energy and
Equity* (1974). *Medical Nemesis / Limits to Medicine* (1975). *Disabling Professions* (1977).

**Serious objections.** The alternatives are thin. Learning webs were sketched, never built at
scale, and it is unclear what replaces mass schooling without worsening inequality.
Anti-institutionalism cuts both ways, since dismantling public provision hands the ground to
markets, and parts of Illich were later adopted enthusiastically by deregulation advocates he
would have opposed. His medical critique, applied literally, has been used to justify rejecting
effective treatment. And an intellectually independent polymath underestimates how much
institutional scaffolding most people need.

**Where to go next.** Paulo Freire, *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* (1968), the constructive
counterpart: banking education, meaning depositing information into passive receptacles, versus
*conscientização*, critical consciousness. Everett Reimer, *School Is Dead*. E. F. Schumacher,
*Small Is Beautiful*. Goffman on total institutions (section 4.9) as the microphysics that
pairs with this.

## 2.6 Giorgio Agamben (born 1942, Italy)

**Position.** The synthesizer. Takes Schmitt's sovereign who decides the exception, fuses it
with Foucault's biopolitics, adds Walter Benjamin and Hannah Arendt, and produces a single
thesis about the structure of Western politics.

**Milieu.** Italian philosophy after the *anni di piombo*, the years of lead, when the Italian
state governed terrorism through emergency legislation and preventive detention. Like Schmitt,
he is generalizing from a specific national experience of emergency government, and that should
be said rather than hidden.

**State of Exception (2003).** Schmitt said the sovereign decides on the exception. Agamben's
addition is historical: in modernity **the exception ceases to be exceptional**. The temporary
suspension becomes the permanent paradigm of government. The crisis is never declared over,
emergency powers are never returned, and governance by emergency becomes the normal mode of
rule rather than its interruption.

The state of exception is a **zone of indistinction** where law is simultaneously in force and
suspended. It applies precisely by withdrawing, holding the subject in its grip while extending
none of its protections. Agamben calls this **force-of-law without law**: binding legal force
detached from any actual statute, a pure capacity to decide over life wearing the form of law
and none of its substance. Cases: the security state, rule by emergency decree, indefinite
detention, the permanent war on terror.

**Homo Sacer (1995).** The figure on the receiving end, recovered from archaic Roman law.
**Homo sacer**, the sacred man, is defined by paradox: he **may be killed but not sacrificed**.
Anyone may kill him with impunity, since he is outside human law, yet he cannot be used in
religious sacrifice, since he is outside divine law. Doubly excluded, and this exclusion is his
mode of inclusion. He is captured by the law in the very act of being abandoned by it.

**The structural claim.** Sovereign and homo sacer are two poles of one apparatus. The sovereign
is the one for whom all men are potentially homo sacer. Homo sacer is the one with respect to
whom every man acts as sovereign.

**Bare life.** Agamben revives the Greek distinction:

- **zoē**, bare biological life, the fact of living, shared with animals.
- **bios**, qualified life, a form of living, politically and ethically shaped, proper to a
  human in a community.

Radicalizing Foucault: Western politics is founded from the start on the **capture of bare
life**. The original sovereign act is to seize *zoē* and draw it into the political sphere as
the thing politics decides over. **Bare life** (*nuda vita*) is what remains when a human is
reduced to *zoē* alone: a living body stripped of political quality, exposed to a power that can
dispose of it without committing either crime or sacrifice. Modern biopolitics is where this
hidden foundation surfaces, since what politics directly administers and optimizes is
increasingly bare life itself.

**The camp as the nomos of the modern.** The concentration camp is not a historical aberration
but the matrix, the pure form of what appears whenever the state of exception is made permanent
and given stable territory: a space where bare life and suspended law enter total indistinction.
Agamben extends the figure, controversially, to refugee camps, migrant detention zones, and
other thresholds where people are held inside a legal order precisely by being placed outside
its protection.

**The mandatory case study, Agamben on COVID.** During the pandemic Agamben published a series
of widely condemned interventions reading public-health measures as the naked face of the
permanent state of exception, equating quarantine and distancing with the logic of the camp.
Many readers, including sympathetic ones, judged this a category error: an all-explaining
apparatus unable to distinguish a contestable public-health measure from totalitarian capture,
because once you own the hammer everything looks like the exception.

**This belongs on the site as a featured worked example.** It is the cleanest available
demonstration of how formidable critique curdles into monism (section 0.3, failure mode B). The
strong use of these tools keeps the lens and keeps the discipline to ask whether the lens fits
this case.

**Primary works.** *Homo Sacer* (1995). *State of Exception* (2003). *Remnants of Auschwitz*
(1998). *What Is an Apparatus?* (2006).

**Serious objections.** Historically and legally imprecise. Legal scholars argue he conflates
constitutionally regulated emergency powers with lawless rule, and flattens vast differences
between detention regimes. The camp as paradigm claim risks trivializing the Holocaust by
generalizing it. Politically it can produce quietism, because if everything is already the camp,
ordinary legal and political remedies look pointless.

**Where to go next.** Walter Benjamin, *Critique of Violence* (1921), on law-making versus
law-preserving violence, and the source of Agamben's dialogue with Schmitt. Hannah Arendt, *The
Origins of Totalitarianism*, on statelessness and the right to have rights. Judith Butler,
*Precarious Life*, as a counter-model. Mbembe, "Necropolitics" (section 4.8), for the colonial
version.

# PART THREE: DOMAINS OF CONTROL

## 3.1 Education and the curriculum question

Because the Prussian claim is the most commonly mangled in the whole field, it needs care.

**The defensible version.** The nineteenth-century Prussian system was explicitly two-track.

- The **Gymnasium**, humanistic and autonomy-forming, on the Humboldtian model of *Bildung*,
  the cultivation of the whole self, for the administrative and professional elite.
- The **Volksschule**, an elementary mass track oriented toward literacy, punctuality,
  obedience, and readiness for industry and conscription.

Compulsory attendance, age-graded cohorts, standardized curricula, teacher certification and
the bell-timed day were Prussian administrative innovations. Horace Mann visited in 1843 and
imported the mass-schooling logic to Massachusetts, from where it spread through the United
States and beyond. Japan's Meiji reformers and much of Latin America drew on the same template.

**The unsupportable version** to avoid: that a specific cabal designed schooling to produce
obedient factory workers as a stated conspiracy. The honest formulation is that a state facing
conscription needs, industrial labour demand, and the problem of governing a mass population
built an institution whose form solved those problems, and that the form outlived the original
purpose and now reproduces itself for reasons nobody consciously chose. Mumford on the
monastery clock (section 4.7) is the deeper version of the same argument about the timetable.

**Scholarly treatments.**

- **Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis, *Schooling in Capitalist America* (1976).** The
  *correspondence principle*: the social relations of school (hierarchy, alienated work for
  external reward, fragmented tasks, competition) mirror the social relations of the workplace,
  so school reproduces the workforce not through curriculum content but through structural
  habituation. *Objection to include:* criticized as economically determinist and for
  underweighting student resistance, and the authors themselves later revised.
- **Philip Jackson, *Life in Classrooms* (1968).** Coined **hidden curriculum**: the lessons
  taught by school's form rather than its syllabus, summarized as crowds, praise and power.
  Learn to wait, to be evaluated, to accept unequal authority.
- **Jean Anyon, "Social Class and the Hidden Curriculum of Work" (1980).** Empirical fieldwork
  showing that schools serving different class communities teach different relationships to
  knowledge and authority. Working-class schools emphasize rote procedure, affluent
  professional schools emphasize creativity and negotiation, elite schools emphasize analytic
  reasoning and rule-making. The single most usable study here for a general audience.
- **John Taylor Gatto, *The Underground History of American Education* and *Dumbing Us Down*.**
  The popular polemical entry point. **Treat as provocation to verify, not source to trust.**
  Gatto's rhetorical force is real, his historical sourcing is loose, and some claims do not
  survive checking. Naming this openly is a credibility asset.

See also Bourdieu (2.1) and Illich (2.5), who carry the theoretical weight in this domain.

## 3.2 The manufacture of consent

Historically the richest seam and the most accessible entry point for new readers. Read this
section alongside Part Four, section 4.2, which supplies the funding history that explains why
American communications research turned out the way it did.

- **Walter Lippmann, *Public Opinion* (1922).** Coined **the manufacture of consent**. Argues
  the public is necessarily governed by **stereotypes**, the pictures in our heads, because the
  real environment is too large and complex to know directly. His conclusion is elitist rather
  than critical: since the bewildered herd cannot govern itself competently, a specialized class
  must manage opinion. Read as an admission by an insider.
- **Edward Bernays, *Propaganda* (1928) and *Crystallizing Public Opinion* (1923).** Freud's
  nephew, the literal inventor of public relations. Opens by stating outright that the
  conscious, intelligent manipulation of the organized habits and opinions of the masses is an
  essential element of democratic society, performed by an invisible governing body. Applied
  psychoanalytic ideas to markets: sell not the product but the desire. His Torches of Freedom
  campaign attached cigarettes to women's emancipation. The primary source that makes the whole
  field concrete, because nothing needs to be exposed. He published the method proudly.
- **Jacques Ellul, *Propaganda: The Formation of Men's Attitudes* (1962).** The deepest and
  least comfortable. Propaganda is not primarily lies, it is a sociological condition of
  technological mass society. It works best on **the educated**, who consume the most
  information, believe themselves immune, and possess the strongest need for coherent opinions
  on everything. Distinguishes *agitation* propaganda from **integration** propaganda, the slow
  kind that produces conformity to an existing order and is far more important. Essential
  correction to any reading that treats propaganda as something done by governments to stupid
  people.
- **Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky, *Manufacturing Consent* (1988).** The **propaganda model**
  and its five filters: ownership, advertising as the revenue source, sourcing dependence on
  official channels, flak meaning organized negative response, and an external enemy or
  ideological frame. The key claim is structural, not conspiratorial: no orders are given, and
  people who internalize the constraints are the ones who rise. **Objections to include:**
  criticized as functionalist and hard to falsify, and for underweighting audience
  interpretation and internal press conflict. The media economy it describes, of mass
  advertising and few outlets, has changed substantially since 1988, and the platform-era update
  is a live question rather than a settled one.
- **Stuart Ewen, *Captains of Consciousness* (1976) and *PR! A Social History of Spin* (1996).**
  The historical record of how advertising set out to produce consumers as a social type.
- **Neil Postman, *Amusing Ourselves to Death* (1985).** The Huxley-not-Orwell argument: the
  danger is not a boot but a medium that renders serious public discourse impossible by
  converting everything into entertainment. Pair with Guy Debord, *The Society of the Spectacle*
  (1967).
- **Adam Curtis** documentaries (*The Century of the Self* on Bernays, *HyperNormalisation*,
  *Can't Get You Out of My Head*). The best-known visual entry point, and also a case study in
  the failure mode: exhilarating, associative, and frequently criticized for implying causation
  through juxtaposition. Recommend with the caveat attached.

## 3.3 Law: foundations and the critique of neutrality

- **Max Weber.** The three types of legitimate authority (traditional, charismatic and
  **rational-legal**) and the routinization of charisma. Modern legal authority is legitimated
  by procedure rather than by justice, which is precisely what makes procedural correctness feel
  like fairness.
- **American Legal Realism** (Oliver Wendell Holmes, Karl Llewellyn, Jerome Frank, 1920s to
  1930s). Law is not deduced from rules; judges decide and then justify. Law as the prophecies
  of what the courts will do in fact. The first systematic break with law as logic.
- **Critical Legal Studies** (Roberto Unger, Duncan Kennedy, Morton Horwitz, 1970s to 1980s).
  The realist insight radicalized: legal doctrine is **indeterminate**, since competing
  principles can justify opposite outcomes in most hard cases, so what settles them is politics
  and power, and rights discourse can entrench as much as it liberates. **Objection to include:**
  the indeterminacy thesis is overstated, since most law is not litigated because most outcomes
  are in fact predictable, and critics argue CLS mistook the hard cases for the whole.
- **Schmitt** (2.3), the pre-legal decision at the foundation.
- **Walter Benjamin, *Critique of Violence* (1921).** The distinction between **law-making**
  violence, the founding act that establishes an order and is itself outside any law, and
  **law-preserving** violence, the ongoing enforcement that maintains it. Every legal order has
  a violent origin it must forget in order to appear legitimate. Tilly (section 4.12) makes the
  same argument with state-formation evidence.
- **Agamben** (2.6), the exception as permanent.
- **Where to go next.** Critical Race Theory as the descendant of CLS applied to race (Derrick
  Bell, Kimberlé Crenshaw). Robert Cover, "Violence and the Word", the short devastating essay
  on the fact that legal interpretation always occurs on a field of pain and death.

## 3.4 Psychological underpinnings

The phrase "dark psychology" is retired here (section 0.3). What follows carries the actual
weight, and every item is empirically contestable, which is the point. The institutional history
of most of this material is in Part Four, section 4.10.

**Compliance and influence**

- **Robert Cialdini, *Influence* (1984).** Reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social
  proof, authority, liking, scarcity, later unity. Written as a practitioner's field guide and
  best read defensively, since it documents what the persuasion industry already does.
- **Robert Jay Lifton, *Thought Reform and the Psychology of Totalism* (1961).** The eight
  criteria of thought reform: milieu control, mystical manipulation, the demand for purity,
  confession, sacred science, loading the language, doctrine over person, and dispensing of
  existence. The most precise available toolkit for identifying coercive persuasion, and it
  applies well beyond cults. **Loading the language**, meaning thought-terminating clichés that
  shut down analysis, is the single most useful concept here for media literacy.

**Conformity and obedience, with mandatory caveats**

- **Solomon Asch (1951).** Conformity to visibly wrong group judgments. Replicates reasonably
  well, with effect sizes varying by culture and era.
- **Stanley Milgram (1961 to 1963).** Obedience to authority. **The caveats are essential.**
  Archival work, notably Gina Perry's *Behind the Shock Machine*, shows the experimenter
  improvised prods well beyond protocol, many participants doubted the setup's reality, and
  reported compliance figures were selectively presented. The finding survives in weakened form.
  The popular version does not.
- **Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment (1971).** **Present as a cautionary tale about
  evidence, not as a finding.** Archival recordings show guards were coached toward brutality,
  the study was never properly replicated, and it functions today mainly as a demonstration of
  how a compelling narrative outruns its data. Including this is a rigor signal.

**Why people defend systems that disadvantage them**

- **System justification theory (John Jost).** Documented tendency to defend and rationalize the
  status quo, sometimes more strongly among the disadvantaged. The closest experimental analogue
  to Gramscian hegemony and Bourdieusian misrecognition.
- **Just-world hypothesis (Melvin Lerner).** The need to believe outcomes are deserved,
  producing victim-blaming and self-blame.
- **Cognitive dissonance (Leon Festinger).** The mechanism by which forced compliance becomes
  sincere belief. Having acted, we revise belief to match the act. This is the experimental
  micro-version of Havel's greengrocer (Part Five).
- **Learned helplessness (Martin Seligman).** The empirical form of Illich's disabling
  professions.

**Attention, framing and choice architecture**

- **Kahneman and Tversky.** Heuristics, biases, and **framing effects**: identical information in
  different frames produces different decisions. Note the replication crisis caveat, since
  priming results in particular have fared badly.
- **Thaler and Sunstein, *Nudge* (2008).** **Read against the grain.** Presented as benevolent
  libertarian paternalism, it is simultaneously a manual for behaviour modification by states
  and corporations, and the most direct contemporary instance of Foucault's governmentality: not
  forcing, but structuring the choice field so free subjects choose as designed. The
  institutional trail is in section 4.10.
- **Shoshana Zuboff, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019).** Behavioural surplus,
  prediction products, and the shift from predicting behaviour to shaping it. The present-day
  version of the Panopticon, with the important difference that the watching is not felt at all.
  **Objection to include:** criticized for technological determinism and for treating an
  advertising business model as a total civilizational rupture.

**The Frankfurt School layer**

- **Adorno and Horkheimer, *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1944).** The **culture industry**: mass
  culture as standardized industrial production that pacifies rather than liberates, offering
  pseudo-individuality and turning leisure into an extension of work. The institutional context
  for how this was written, and what happened when Adorno collided with American audience
  research, is in section 4.2, which is the more useful entry point.
- **Herbert Marcuse, *One-Dimensional Man* (1964).** **Repressive tolerance** and **false
  needs**: an affluent society that satisfies manufactured desires and absorbs opposition,
  producing subjects unable to imagine alternatives. Note the paternalism objection, since
  someone has to decide which needs are false.

## 3.5 Bureaucracy, measurement and legibility

The domain most visible in everyday life and therefore the best on-ramp for a younger audience.

- **Max Weber.** Bureaucracy as the most technically efficient form of organization and as the
  **iron cage**: rationalization producing a disenchanted world of specialists without spirit.
- **James C. Scott, *Seeing Like a State* (1998).** **Legibility.** States cannot govern what
  they cannot read, so they simplify reality into countable, mappable, taxable form: cadastral
  surveys, permanent surnames, standardized weights, grid cities, scientific forestry. The
  simplification is not neutral, because reality gets reshaped to match the map. Combined with
  high-modernist ideology, authoritarian power and a prostrate civil society, this produces
  catastrophe. His **mētis**, meaning practical local unformalizable knowledge, is the thing
  legibility destroys and the direct ancestor of Illich's conviviality. Also *Domination and the
  Arts of Resistance* (1990): **public transcript** versus **hidden transcript**, and
  infrapolitics, the disguised everyday resistance that precedes open revolt. This is the
  strongest available answer to the agency problem in Bourdieu and Foucault. Pair with Bowker
  and Star on classification (section 4.12).
- **David Graeber, *The Utopia of Rules* (2015).** The **iron law of liberalism**: market
  reforms and deregulation reliably increase total bureaucracy. Structural stupidity produced by
  asymmetric power, since those below must interpret those above and those above need not
  reciprocate. Also *Bullshit Jobs* (2018) and *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011), which
  dismantles the barter-origin myth of money and shows debt's role as a moral language of
  obligation.
- **Goodhart's Law.** When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The most
  portable idea in this section and the operational core of what goes wrong with metrics in
  schools, hospitals, policing and platforms. Pair with **Campbell's Law** and Jerry Muller,
  *The Tyranny of Metrics* (2018).
- **C. Wright Mills, *The Power Elite* (1956).** Interlocking military, corporate and political
  leadership as a coherent stratum by shared background and circulation rather than by
  conspiracy. Descends directly from the Italian elite theorists (section 4.5). And ***The
  Sociological Imagination* (1959)**, the discipline of connecting **personal troubles** to
  **public issues**, which is the founding intellectual move of this entire project and should
  probably open the website.

# PART FOUR: MILIEUS

Organized by setting rather than by person, following the method in section 0.2. Each cluster
gives the setting, the years, the money, who was in the room, what came out of it, and the
specific insight to take.

## 4.1 Cybernetics and the Macy Conferences

**Setting.** New York, 1946 to 1953. Ten conferences funded by the Josiah Macy Jr. Foundation,
a medical philanthropy. Interdisciplinary by design and small by design.

**Climate.** Directly out of the war. Wiener's wartime work was an anti-aircraft predictor:
model the pilot's evasive behaviour well enough to shoot him down. The founding gesture of
cybernetics is treating a human being as a predictable feedback system under fire. That origin
never fully washes out of the field.

**Who was in the room.** Norbert Wiener (MIT), John von Neumann, Warren McCulloch (chair),
Walter Pitts, Claude Shannon, Gregory Bateson, Margaret Mead, Heinz von Foerster, Kurt Lewin.
Anthropologists and psychiatrists sat with mathematicians and engineers, which is why the
vocabulary of feedback, homeostasis and information spread so fast into the social sciences.

**Key works and the insight.**

- Wiener, *Cybernetics* (1948), and above all *The Human Use of Human Beings* (1950,
  substantially revised 1954). The title is a double edge. Wiener built the science of control
  through feedback, then wrote the warning: a society that treats people as components in a
  control loop, managed by information they do not hold, degrades them into messages in someone
  else's system. He argued that information wants to circulate and that secrecy and command
  hierarchy are entropic. Read the 1950 and 1954 editions against each other, since the second
  is more pessimistic and the changes are informative.
- Wiener refused military funding after 1947 and published his refusal ("A Scientist Rebels",
  *Atlantic*, January 1947). A rare, documented case of a founder trying to withdraw a
  technology from the institution that paid for it.
- Bateson, *Steps to an Ecology of Mind* (1972). Feedback applied to families, schizophrenia
  and communication, and the double bind as a control structure with no exit.
- von Foerster, second-order cybernetics: the observer is inside the system being observed.

**What it adds.** The engineering layer beneath the philosophy of invisible power. Foucault
describes discipline; cybernetics supplies the mechanism, feedback and the servo loop, plus a
first-generation insider's alarm about applying it to people.

**Institutional history.** Steve Joshua Heims, *The Cybernetics Group* (1991). Ronald Kline,
*The Cybernetics Moment* (2015).

## 4.2 Administrative versus critical research: the Bureau and the Radio Project

**Setting.** Princeton then Columbia, 1937 into the 1940s. The Princeton Radio Research Project,
funded by the Rockefeller Foundation, became Paul Lazarsfeld's Bureau of Applied Social Research
at Columbia.

**Climate.** Radio was the first mass medium suspected of being able to move a whole population,
and the sponsors wanted measurement, not critique. This is the origin point of audience
research, the focus group, the panel study and the two-step flow model.

**Why this cluster matters more than any single figure.** Lazarsfeld hired Theodor Adorno to run
the music section. The collaboration failed within two years. Adorno would not convert listening
into countable preference data, and Lazarsfeld could not fund what could not be measured.
Lazarsfeld himself named the split, in English, as **administrative research** (done for an
institution, to improve its operation) versus **critical research** (asking what the institution
is for and whom it serves). That failure is the cleanest case study in this whole field of how
funding structure determines findings. The same building, the same data, two incompatible
sciences.

**Key works.**

- Lazarsfeld, "Remarks on Administrative and Critical Communications Research" (1941). Short,
  and the single most useful primary document in this cluster.
- Lazarsfeld, Berelson and Gaudet, *The People's Choice* (1944). Katz and Lazarsfeld, *Personal
  Influence* (1955). The two-step flow, which found media effects to be weaker and more socially
  mediated than the propaganda theorists assumed. Note the convenience of that finding for the
  sponsors and argue with it either way.
- Adorno, "Scientific Experiences of a European Scholar in America" (1969), his own account of
  the collision.

**Institutional history.** David Morrison's work on the Radio Project. Christopher Simpson,
*Science of Coercion* (1994), on how US communication research was shaped by
psychological-warfare funding from 1945 to 1960. Simpson is the key text and is underread.

## 4.3 RAND and the systems-analysis state

**Setting.** Santa Monica, from 1948. Spun out of Douglas Aircraft on US Air Force money as the
first modern think tank.

**Climate.** Nuclear standoff. Problems that could not be experimented on had to be modelled
instead, which put enormous authority into formalism: game theory, operations research, linear
programming, systems analysis.

**Who.** John von Neumann, Albert Wohlstetter, Thomas Schelling, Herman Kahn, Kenneth Arrow,
Bernard Brodie, later Daniel Ellsberg, whose defection makes him the best inside witness.

**The insight.** This is where governance by model begins. Rational-choice assumptions developed
for deterrence migrated into welfare policy, education, policing and management, carrying an
implicit anthropology, the strategic self-interested actor, into domains where nobody had chosen
it. Control here is not surveillance. It is the quiet authority of the model that decides which
options are visible.

**Key works.** Schelling, *The Strategy of Conflict* (1960). Kahn, *On Thermonuclear War*
(1960). Ellsberg, *Secrets* (2002) and *The Doomsday Machine* (2017). For the critique: S. M.
Amadae, *Rationalizing Capitalist Democracy* (2003), on how rational choice became the default
language of legitimacy, and Paul Erickson et al., *How Reason Almost Lost Its Mind* (2013).

## 4.4 The cultural Cold War: Congress for Cultural Freedom

**Setting.** Founded Berlin, 1950. Offices in 35 countries at its peak. Covertly funded by the
CIA through foundation conduits until exposed in 1966 and 1967.

**Climate.** The contest for European intellectuals. The instrument was not censorship but
patronage: magazines (*Encounter*, *Preuves*, *Der Monat*, *Tempo Presente*), conferences,
concerts, exhibitions, prizes, translation subsidies.

**The insight, and it is the sharpest one here.** This is invisible control without a single lie
being told. Most of the writers published were doing honest work and did not know the source of
the money. Power operated by shaping the field: which topics got a well-paid venue, which
journals had money for translation, which careers became viable. It set the range of the sayable
by funding one end of it. Note the counterargument seriously, since several participants insisted
the money changed nothing they wrote, and that claim has to be engaged rather than dismissed.

**This cluster is the historical proof of failure mode A** in section 0.3, and the reason any
organization built out of this research has to think hard about where its money comes from.

**Key works.** Frances Stonor Saunders, *Who Paid the Piper?* (UK) and *The Cultural Cold War*
(US, 1999) is the standard account, and it is contested by participants, so read a critical
review alongside it. Peter Coleman, *The Liberal Conspiracy* (1989), the sympathetic insider
version. Giles Scott-Smith on the politics of apolitical culture.

**Adjacent, same logic.** Ford Foundation area-studies funding. The Farfield Foundation and other
conduits. MKUltra and the wider Cold War behavioural-science programmes, on which John Marks,
*The Search for the Manchurian Candidate* (1979), was built from surviving files after most were
destroyed.

## 4.5 Elite theory: Italy and Germany before fascism

**Setting.** Italy and Germany, roughly 1896 to 1915, under mass-suffrage expansion and party
bureaucratization.

**Climate.** These writers watched democratic forms arrive without democratic substance and built
a general theory from it. Politically they run in several directions, some ugly, which is
precisely why the arguments have to be met rather than avoided.

**The core claims.**

- **Gaetano Mosca**, *The Ruling Class* (1896). Every society is run by an organized minority
  over a disorganized majority, and each ruling class legitimates itself with a "political
  formula" (divine right, the will of the people, expertise) that is believed sincerely and is
  never merely cynical.
- **Vilfredo Pareto**, *The Mind and Society* (1916). "Derivations" are the reasons people give,
  "residues" are the drives underneath. History as the circulation of elites.
- **Robert Michels**, *Political Parties* (1911). The iron law of oligarchy, derived from the
  German SPD, the most internally democratic mass party then in existence. Organization itself
  produces a permanent leadership stratum with interests distinct from the members. The finding
  is structural, which is what makes it devastating.

**Why it belongs.** C. Wright Mills descends directly from this line, as does James Burnham
(*The Managerial Revolution*, 1941, and *The Machiavellians*, 1943). Bertrand de Jouvenel's *On
Power* (1945), written in occupied France, adds the long argument that centralizing power expands
by liberating individuals from intermediate bodies, so emancipation and centralization advance
together. Michels also stands as the standing objection to Gramsci (section 2.2).

## 4.6 The neoliberal thought collective

**Setting.** Mont Pelerin, Switzerland, April 1947, then a fifty-year institutional build-out:
the Institute of Economic Affairs (London), the Virginia School and public choice, the Chicago
School, a network of foundations, and later the WTO and investor-protection architecture.

**Climate.** Defeat. In 1947 this was a marginal group convinced the postwar consensus was
permanent. The interesting question is not the doctrine but how a marginal network became the
default administrative language of states within four decades.

**The insight.** This is the best-documented modern case of deliberate long-horizon institutional
capture, and it directly contradicts the assumption that hegemony is always impersonal drift.
Read the meeting minutes, the funding structures and the personnel flows. Mirowski's argument is
that the movement's distinctive achievement was using state power to build and defend markets
while speaking a language of state retreat.

Because this is the one cluster where the conspiracy-shaped reading is partly correct, it needs
the highest evidentiary standard rather than the lowest. Coordination is documented. Omnipotence
is not.

**Key works.** Philip Mirowski and Dieter Plehwe (eds.), *The Road from Mont Pèlerin* (2009), and
Mirowski, *Never Let a Serious Crisis Go to Waste* (2013). Quinn Slobodian, *Globalists* (2018),
on the Geneva School designing supranational insulation of markets from democratic majorities,
which is the most relevant strand here: control by removing questions from the domain of
decision. Angus Burgin, *The Great Persuasion* (2012).

**Counterpoint, and essential.** Karl Polanyi, *The Great Transformation* (1944): the
self-regulating market was itself an act of state construction, not a natural state. Nancy
MacLean, *Democracy in Chains* (2017) on James Buchanan is vigorously contested, and if used,
use it with the rebuttals.

## 4.7 Media materialism: Toronto, Berlin, Paris

**Setting.** Three distinct scenes, one shared claim: the medium's physical properties structure
what a society can think and who can rule it.

- **Toronto, 1950s.** Harold Innis, *Empire and Communications* (1950) and *The Bias of
  Communication* (1951). Innis was a political economist of staples (fur, timber, cod) before he
  turned to media, which is why his account is materialist. Media biased toward **time** (stone,
  parchment, oral tradition) sustain religion, tradition and continuity. Media biased toward
  **space** (papyrus, print, telegraph) sustain administration, empire and territorial control.
  Monopolies of knowledge form around a dominant medium and are broken by a new one. Innis is
  sharper and less quoted than McLuhan, who followed him at Toronto.
- **Berlin, 1980s to 2000s.** Friedrich Kittler, *Gramophone, Film, Typewriter* (1986) and
  *Discourse Networks 1800/1900*. Media as hardware with military ancestry. Media determine our
  situation. Deliberately anti-humanist, and useful as a corrective when a media argument gets
  too comfortable.
- **Paris.** Paul Virilio, *Speed and Politics* (1977), and dromology: political power as the
  control of speed and circulation, the state as an accelerator, the accident as the truth of
  the technology. Vilém Flusser, *Towards a Philosophy of Photography* (1983): the apparatus
  programmes the user who believes they are using it.

**Older and larger.** Lewis Mumford, *Technics and Civilization* (1934) and *The Myth of the
Machine* (1967 and 1970). The **megamachine**: the first machine in history was made of human
parts, organized to build the pyramids, and the distinction that matters is authoritarian versus
democratic technics. Also Mumford on the monastery clock as the decisive machine of the
industrial age, which is the concrete version of the timetable argument in section 3.1 and in
Foucault.

**One short essay to read now.** Langdon Winner, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (1980). Whether
technical arrangements embody political orders, argued with concrete cases.

## 4.8 Control after discipline

**Setting.** France and Britain, roughly 1990 onward. Written as the disciplinary institutions
Foucault described, meaning factory, asylum and school as enclosure, were visibly dissolving.

**The insight.** Gilles Deleuze, "Postscript on the Societies of Control" (1990), six pages, the
single highest-yield short text in this whole document. Discipline operated by **enclosure** and
moulds, so you passed from family to school to barracks to factory, each a separate casting.
Control operates by **modulation**: continuous assessment, perpetual training, rolling credit, no
graduation and no discharge. The factory was a body, the corporation is a gas. The signature of
discipline is the signature and the file; of control, the password and the code. The individual
becomes a **dividual**, split into data attributes. Written before the web and almost unnervingly
accurate about it.

**Follow-ons.**

- Agamben (section 2.6), the exception as the normal technique of government.
- Nikolas Rose, *Governing the Soul* (1989) and *Powers of Freedom* (1999). How psychology as a
  discipline made the self governable, and how advanced-liberal rule works through freedom,
  choice and self-management rather than against them. Rose and Peter Miller, "Political Power
  beyond the State" (1992), is the compact statement. This is the most useful extension of
  Foucault's governmentality.
- Wendy Brown, *Undoing the Demos* (2015). The conversion of citizens into human capital and of
  political questions into questions of competitiveness.
- Achille Mbembe, "Necropolitics" (2003). What biopower looks like from the colony rather than
  the metropole, which is where these techniques were often tested first. Pair with section 8.5.
- Byung-Chul Han, *The Burnout Society* (2010) and *Psychopolitics* (2014). The shift from
  disciplinary society to **achievement society**, where the subject exploits itself voluntarily
  and calls it freedom. The most direct update of this material for a young audience and probably
  the best single contemporary hook for the site.

## 4.9 Institutions as manufacturers of persons

**Setting.** Postwar North America, sociology and the critique of the service professions.

- **Erving Goffman**, *Asylums* (1961), based on fieldwork at St Elizabeths Hospital in
  Washington. The **total institution** and the moral career of the inmate: how an organization
  strips and rebuilds a self through admission procedures, and how the underlife of small evasions
  is part of the system rather than a failure of it. Also *The Presentation of Self in Everyday
  Life* (1956) and *Stigma* (1963). Goffman is the microphysics that pairs with Foucault's
  macrophysics and is much easier to test against your own experience of any institution.
- **Ivan Illich.** Full treatment in section 2.5. His milieu belongs here: CIDOC in Cuernavaca,
  funded by language-course fees rather than foundations, which is why he could write what he
  wrote.
- **Norbert Elias**, *The Civilizing Process* (1939, written in exile, ignored until the 1970s).
  External restraint becomes self-restraint as state monopolies on violence and taxation lengthen
  the chains of interdependence. The long-run history of how control moves from the outside to
  the inside of a person, which is exactly the panopticon claim made over five centuries and with
  evidence.

## 4.10 The behavioural state, then and now

**Setting.** From behaviourism to the nudge unit, with a live institutional record. This is the
institutional history behind section 3.4.

- **B. F. Skinner**, *Beyond Freedom and Dignity* (1971) and *Walden Two* (1948). Argued openly
  for designing environments that produce desired behaviour and dismissed autonomy as
  pre-scientific. Valuable because he states without embarrassment what later programmes do
  quietly.
- **Tavistock Institute** (London, 1946, initial Rockefeller funding). Group relations, human
  relations in industry, applied psychoanalysis to organizations. Attracts a large amount of
  conspiracy literature, which is a reason to use its own publications and serious histories
  rather than secondary claims.
- **Behavioural Insights Team** (UK Cabinet Office, 2010, later part-privatized). The first state
  nudge unit, and dozens of governments copied it. There is a published trail: the MINDSPACE
  report (2010), the EAST framework, randomized trials. Read Thaler and Sunstein, *Nudge* (2008),
  as a primary document of governing technique rather than as advice, and note the internal
  critique that libertarian paternalism relies on the citizen not noticing the architecture.
- **Contemporary and algorithmic.** Virginia Eubanks, *Automating Inequality* (2018), on how
  automated eligibility and risk scoring is tested on the poor first. Frank Pasquale, *The Black
  Box Society* (2015), on authority that cannot be inspected. Cathy O'Neil, *Weapons of Math
  Destruction* (2016). Safiya Noble, *Algorithms of Oppression* (2018). These pair with Zuboff
  (section 3.4) and with Deleuze's modulation (section 4.8).

## 4.11 Deeper anchors: pre-twentieth century and early crowd theory

- **Étienne de La Boétie**, *Discourse on Voluntary Servitude* (c. 1548, written at about
  eighteen, circulated in manuscript). The founding question of this entire field: not why
  tyrants rule, but why millions obey when the tyrant has only the power they lend him. Withdraw
  consent and he falls, no violence needed. Everything from Gramsci to Gene Sharp is downstream,
  and Havel (Part Five) is the twentieth-century restatement.
- **Gabriel Tarde**, *L'opinion et la foule* (1901). Argued against Le Bon that the modern
  formation is not the physical crowd but the **public**, dispersed and held together only by
  simultaneous reading of the same newspaper. He identified the mediated public decades before
  broadcast, and has been substantially rehabilitated by later network theory.
- **Gustave Le Bon**, *The Crowd* (1895). Weak as science, historically decisive. Read by
  Mussolini, by Hitler, and by the founders of public relations. Included because of its effect,
  not its truth.
- **Serge Chakhotin**, *The Rape of the Masses* (1939). A Pavlov student who worked on anti-Nazi
  symbol campaigns for the German SPD and analysed propaganda as conditioned reflex. A rare
  technician's account written by someone using the techniques defensively.
- **Thorstein Veblen**, *The Engineers and the Price System* (1921) and *The Theory of the Leisure
  Class* (1899). Sabotage as a normal business practice, conspicuous consumption as a status
  control mechanism, and the rule of a technical class as a live proposal.

## 4.12 Infrastructural power: how states actually reach people

**The claim.** Much control literature stays at the level of ideology. This cluster asks the
physical question: through what pipes, records and materials does authority actually touch a
body, and what does that infrastructure allow or forbid.

- **Michael Mann**, *The Sources of Social Power* (four volumes, 1986 to 2012). The key
  distinction is **despotic power**, what elites can do without negotiating, versus
  **infrastructural power**, the capacity to actually penetrate society and implement decisions.
  Modern states are historically low on the first and unprecedented on the second, which is the
  sharpest available correction to the assumption that visible authoritarianism is the main
  danger.
- **Charles Tilly**, "War Making and State Making as Organized Crime" (1985) and *Coercion,
  Capital, and European States* (1990). States as protection rackets that succeeded and then
  legitimated themselves retrospectively. The evidentiary version of Benjamin on law-making
  violence (section 3.3).
- **Timothy Mitchell**, *Carbon Democracy* (2011) and *Rule of Experts* (2002). The best single
  argument in this cluster: the material properties of coal, concentrated, moved by rail,
  requiring dense labour at choke points, enabled mass democracy through the strike, and the
  switch to oil, pumped, piped, shipped and needing few workers, engineered that leverage away.
  Democratic power as a function of infrastructure geometry.
- **Karl Wittfogel**, *Oriental Despotism* (1957). The hydraulic-state thesis, largely rejected
  empirically and shaped by his own break with communism, but historically important and a good
  exercise in reading a discredited argument for what it still gets right.
- **Bruno Latour** and STS generally, for how facts and standards get built and then become
  invisible infrastructure. Pair with Geoffrey Bowker and Susan Leigh Star, *Sorting Things Out*
  (1999), on classification systems as silent political instruments, which connects directly to
  Scott on legibility (section 3.5).

# PART FIVE: EAST AND WEST

The axis with the most immediate purchase for a Central European audience, and it has two
distinct meanings that must not be blurred.

## 5.1 Gramsci's technical distinction

Gramsci's East and West is about the thickness of civil society, and it produces his central
strategic conclusion.

- **In the East**, meaning Tsarist Russia, the state was everything and civil society was
  primordial and gelatinous. A frontal assault, a **war of maneuver**, could seize power in a
  single blow. October 1917 worked because there was little between the state and the population
  to absorb the shock.
- **In the West**, civil society is thick, a powerful system of fortresses and earthworks
  standing behind the state. Frontal assault fails there, and Gramsci had watched it fail across
  postwar Europe. The task is a long **war of position**: patient struggle inside civil society,
  in schools, press, publishing and culture, to win intellectual ground and build a rival
  worldview, as the precondition of political power. **You have to win the culture before you can
  win the state.**

Note for anyone building an institute in this tradition: that is a war-of-position project by
definition, whether or not the builders intend it. Note also that section 4.6 describes a
successful fifty-year war of position run from the right, which is the best available empirical
test of the strategy.

## 5.2 The second contrast: coercion-based versus consent-based control

The twentieth century ran, in effect, a controlled experiment. Two regimes pursuing social
control by opposite primary means, on the same continent, at the same time. Each is best
understood through the other.

| | **Eastern bloc, state-socialist** | **Western liberal-capitalist** |
|---|---|---|
| Primary instrument | Coercion, with consent secondary | Consent, with coercion in reserve |
| Where truth is managed | Central censorship, a visible office deciding what may be printed | Market and institutional filtering, no office, but ownership, advertising dependence, sourcing routines and career incentives shape what gets said |
| Relation to ideology | Explicit, official, compulsory, widely disbelieved | Implicit, unofficial, optional, widely believed |
| Characteristic subject | The dissembler, who says the required words, believes none of them, and knows others do not either | The sincere participant, who is not required to say anything and genuinely holds the ambient worldview |
| Failure mode | Legitimacy collapse. Everyone knows the emperor is naked, and the system rots and falls fast | Legitimacy without alternatives. No collapse point, because dissent is absorbed, marketed and monetized |
| How dissent is handled | Suppression, prison, exile | Absorption. Made into a niche, a genre, a product, a career |

**The uncomfortable conclusion**, and the reason this comparison earns its place: **a system that
must lie to you openly is easier to see through than one that does not have to lie at all.**
Overt censorship produces a population fluent in reading between lines. A consent-based order
produces a population with no practice at it, because nothing appears to be hidden.

Section 4.4 is the bridge between the two columns. The Congress for Cultural Freedom is the West
doing censorship's job through patronage, which is the same outcome reached by the other method.

## 5.3 The Central European canon, largely missing from Western syllabi

- **Václav Havel, *The Power of the Powerless* (1978).** The single best text on the East side.
  The greengrocer places the sign "Workers of the world, unite!" in his window. He does not
  believe it, nobody thinks he does, and nobody reads it. The sign's real message is that he is
  obedient and therefore has the right to be left alone. This is **post-totalitarianism**, a
  system running not on belief but on **living within a lie**, in which each person's ritual
  compliance becomes the pressure that holds the next person in place. Everyone is simultaneously
  victim and enforcer. The counter-move is **living in truth**, refusing the ritual, which is
  dangerous precisely because it exposes the lie as a lie. Pair directly with Gramsci and
  Bourdieu, since Havel describes hegemony without belief, which is the limit case that tests
  both theories. Pair also with Festinger (section 3.4) and La Boétie (section 4.11).
- **Czesław Miłosz, *The Captive Mind* (1953).** The psychology of intellectual accommodation
  under an imposed ideology. Introduces **ketman**, borrowed from Persian practice: the elaborate
  internal art of professing the required doctrine while maintaining private reservation, and the
  way this self-division eventually consumes the self it was meant to protect. The finest
  psychological study of complicity in the literature.
- **György Konrád and Iván Szelényi, *The Intellectuals on the Road to Class Power* (1979).**
  Written in Hungary. Argues that under state socialism the intelligentsia was itself becoming a
  ruling class through monopoly over teleological knowledge, meaning planning expertise as the
  basis of domination. A structural critique of expert rule from inside the East, and the natural
  bridge to Western critiques of technocracy in sections 4.3 and 4.5. Konrád and Szelényi reach
  Burnham's managerial-revolution conclusion from the opposite side of the Iron Curtain, which is
  a convergence worth a section of its own.
- **István Bibó**, on the political culture and hysteria of small Eastern European states.
  Indispensable for the regional frame.
- **Alexander Zinoviev, *Homo Sovieticus* (1982).** Satirical anthropology of the character type
  the system produced.
- **Tony Judt, *Postwar* (2005).** The reliable single-volume history to ground all of it.
- **Ivan Krastev and Stephen Holmes, *The Light That Failed* (2019).** The post-1989 synthesis:
  the politics of imitation, what it does to a society to be told its task is to become a copy of
  somewhere else, and how resentment of that imposed imitation shaped the region's subsequent
  politics. The most direct link between the two halves of this section and current events.

## 5.4 What each side reveals about the other

- The East demonstrates that **control does not require belief**. Ritual compliance is
  sufficient, and may be more stable than sincerity, until it is not.
- The West demonstrates that **control does not require an office**. Dispersed incentives produce
  coordinated outcomes with no coordinator, which is exactly Foucault's capillary power and
  Herman and Chomsky's filters.
- Together they refute the two lazy readings: that control requires a conspiracy, since the East
  had an actual apparatus and still lost the population's belief, and that the absence of a
  conspiracy means the absence of control, since the West has no apparatus and holds belief far
  more securely.

# PART SIX: THE COUNTER-TRADITION

Analysis without a constructive edge produces the paralysis Agamben is accused of. Anything built
from this research needs this part or it reads as despair.

- **Mills.** Personal troubles into public issues, the reframing that turns private failure into
  a shared structural condition.
- **La Boétie.** The tyrant has only the power you lend him. The oldest and simplest version.
- **Freire.** *Conscientização*: literacy taught as the reading of one's own social situation, and
  the shift from banking education to dialogue.
- **Gramsci.** The war of position, organic intellectuals, the long build inside civil society.
- **Illich.** Conviviality, and keeping tools below the threshold at which they enforce
  dependence. The most directly usable design brief in the document.
- **Wiener.** A founder publicly refusing the funding that would have directed his work, in
  print, in 1947. A concrete precedent for institutional independence rather than a theory of it.
- **Havel.** Living in truth. The greengrocer who takes the sign down.
- **Scott.** *Mētis* and the hidden transcript, the defense of practical local knowledge and
  everyday resistance.
- **Bourdieu.** Reflexivity: turning the analysis on yourself and your own position first, which
  is also the discipline that prevents this material from becoming a way of feeling superior.

# PART EIGHT: OPEN QUESTIONS

Ready to be worked on. Each is a section that does not exist yet.

## 8.1 Personnel flow, 1946 to 1960

The Macy Conferences and the Bureau of Applied Social Research overlapped in people and period.
Map the personnel flow between cybernetics, communications research and psychological warfare.
Simpson does part of this and it is incomplete.

## 8.2 Tarde's public under recommender systems

Tarde's *public* versus Le Bon's *crowd* is a live distinction again under algorithmic media.
Nobody has fully redone Tarde with recommender systems in view. Tarde's public was constituted by
simultaneous reading of the same newspaper; the algorithmic public is constituted by
non-simultaneous reading of different feeds, which may break his mechanism entirely or may be its
purest form.

## 8.3 Radical monopoly and the platforms

Illich's radical monopoly is the strongest unused concept for platform analysis, sharper than most
contemporary monopoly language, and largely absent from that debate. Antitrust asks whether a firm
has excluded competitors. Radical monopoly asks whether a mode of provision has abolished the
alternatives, which is a different and more relevant question when the issue is that there is no
longer a non-platform way to do a thing.

## 8.4 Patronage control, then and now

The Congress for Cultural Freedom model, meaning control by patronage rather than censorship, has
an obvious contemporary analogue in research funding, philanthropic media grants and platform
monetization. The comparison has not been done rigorously. It is also the question any organization
built from this research has to answer about itself.

## 8.5 The colonial angle

Every cluster in Part Four is North Atlantic. The colonial testing ground argument, following
Mbembe and Mitchell on Egypt, suggests the techniques were often developed at the periphery and
reimported. A version of this document built from that angle would look different, and it is the
largest single gap in the present draft.

## 8.6 Gramsci versus Michels, unresolved

Gramsci's answer to hegemony is counter-hegemonic organization. Michels' finding is that
organization itself generates an oligarchy with interests distinct from its members, derived from
the most internally democratic party then in existence. Both cannot be fully right. Nobody has
adequately worked the tension, and it bears directly on anything built out of this material.

# APPENDIX A: THE ORGANIZATIONAL LANDSCAPE

Recorded for completeness. Largely a map of what a project built from this research is not.

**Existing youth think-tank networks in Europe.**

- **foraus** (Switzerland, founded 2009). The grassroots think tank template. Participatory,
  non-partisan, volunteer-run, regional branches in university cities, elected board and general
  assembly, roughly 1,000 members. Founder Nicola Forster, an Ashoka Fellow, built it on the
  observation that young people are interested in issue politics rather than party politics.
  Developed **Policy Kitchen**, a crowdsourcing method combining a digital platform with physical
  workshops of roughly 10 to 40 participants.
- **Polis180** (Berlin, founded 2015). Over 650 members, thematic programmes, a Beyond Berlin
  remote track, funded by donations, membership fees and contributions.
- **Argo** (Paris), **Ponto**, and others, federated in the **Open Think Tank Network**.
- **ThinkYoung** (Brussels, 2009), **European Student Think Tank**, **European Youth Think Tank**,
  **On Think Tanks** and its Building for the Future initiative, and the **European Youth Forum**.

**Funding landscape.** CERV, the Citizens, Equality, Rights and Values programme, funds civil
society at roughly 90 per cent rates for transnational democracy and participation projects.
Erasmus+ and European Youth Together fund youth participation and network-building. National
foundations and party-affiliated foundations. Municipal support for space. **Caveat on the
record:** think tanks have been increasingly deprioritized in EU funding, and the 2026 to 2028
CERV operating grant results showed little support for them.

**Why this route is structurally closed to a project built on Part Four.** An organization
studying how institutions manufacture legitimacy cannot be funded by those institutions. Grant
dependence plus access dependence selects for criticism that never threatens the source. Section
4.4 is what that looks like when it works, and it worked on people who were doing honest work and
did not know where the money came from. The independence-preserving model is audience revenue:
membership dues, patronage, paid seminars and reading-intensive courses, a serious publication,
and books. The form is closer to an institute plus journal plus salon, a reading and research
order, than to a policy NGO or an advocacy organization.

**Design principles.** Non-partisanship written into the statutes rather than the marketing.
Federated local chapters rather than a central headquarters. Low-barrier entry followed by
rigorous output. Senior review pairing for every junior author. A registered non-profit
association with an elected board. Quality control as the actual moat. And the standing objection
from section 4.5, since Michels derived the iron law of oligarchy from exactly this kind of
organization.

# APPENDIX C: PROVENANCE AND STATUS

This document merges two research streams.

**Stream one**, Parts One (0.1 and 0.3), Two, Three, Five, Six, and Appendices A and B, developed
from a conversation on the core thinkers, the domains of control, and the organizational question.

**Stream two**, Parts One (0.2), Four, Seven (7.2) and Eight, developed as a milieu-based research
extension covering what the core line did not: cybernetics, the administrative and critical split,
RAND, the Congress for Cultural Freedom, elite theory, the neoliberal thought collective, media
materialism, control after discipline, institutions as manufacturers of persons, the behavioural
state, pre-twentieth-century anchors, and infrastructural power.

Overlaps between the two streams have been resolved into cross-references rather than repeated.
