Foucault's early empirical site was the psychiatric hospital, and the discovery that
gives his work its charge arrived in the 1960s: the humane, reforming, therapeutic
institutions of the welfare state turned out to be instruments of control in their own
right. Not because anyone was lying about wanting to help.

<Thesis source="Discipline and Punish, 1975">
Modern power does not need to be visible, dramatic or even intended. It needs to be
continuous.
</Thesis>

<Mechanism />

## The historical demonstration

*Discipline and Punish* opens with the public execution and dismemberment of Damiens
the regicide in 1757, in appalling detail, then sets against it a drab prison timetable
from eighty years later. Every hour accounted for, no blood at all.

The obvious reading is that we got kinder. Foucault's reading is that we got a
different and far more efficient technology.

Sovereign power is spectacular, occasional and aimed at the body. It writes the law on
the criminal's flesh where everyone can see, and then it goes away and does not touch
your Tuesday afternoon. <Term id="disciplinary-power">Disciplinary power</Term> is
continuous, invisible and mundane. It aims to produce useful, obedient people through
small constant techniques: the timetable, the drill, ranked rows of desks, the
partitioning of space. It manufactures <Term id="docile-bodies">docile bodies</Term>,
productive and politically harmless at once. Perfected in monasteries, armies and
workshops, then generalized to the school, the hospital and the factory.

## Power and knowledge are one thing

<Term id="power-knowledge">Power/knowledge</Term>, with the slash carrying the
argument. They are not two separate things that meet. There is no exercise of power
without organizing a field of knowledge, and no field of knowledge that does not carry
relations of power inside it. The prison generates criminology, and criminology
underwrites the prison.

This is why neutral expertise deserves attention rather than automatic trust. Not
because experts lie. Because producing the truth about a domain is at the same time
producing power over it.

## Two later widenings

<Term id="biopolitics">Biopolitics</Term>. Alongside discipline, which works on the
individual body, a power aimed at the population as a biological mass: birth rates,
mortality, health, hygiene, the life of the species. The old sovereign right was to
take life or let live. The new formula inverts it, to make live and let die. This is
the door [Agamben](/thinkers/agamben) walks through.

<Term id="governmentality">Governmentality</Term>. Liberal government works less by
crushing freedom than by shaping it, which he calls the conduct of conduct. It
structures the field of possible actions so that free people, doing what feels like
their own choosing, conduct themselves in governable ways. You are not forced. You are
configured to want what keeps the system running. If that sounds like a description of
a well-designed app, that is the point of
[the behavioural state](/milieus/behavioural-state).

## Psychological underpinning

Self-surveillance and internalized evaluation. The confessional mode as a technique for
producing a self that can be inspected. The quiet normalizing force of psychometrics and
diagnostic categories, which do not merely describe people but supply the categories
people then use to understand themselves.

## Primary works

- *Discipline and Punish* (1975). Start here, and read Part Three, "Panopticism", first
  if you only read one chapter.
- *The History of Sexuality, Volume 1* (1976).
- *Madness and Civilization* (1961) and *The Birth of the Clinic* (1963).
- The lecture courses *Security, Territory, Population* and *The Birth of Biopolitics*
  (1978 to 1979), where governmentality is worked out.

<Objections>
- Historically loose in places. Specialists have contested his account of the Great
  Confinement and of how madness was treated before the modern period.
- If power is everywhere and produces the subject, it is unclear what resistance stands
  on, or on what basis anything is called bad. Habermas and Nancy Fraser press this
  normative gap hard and Foucault never fully answered it.
- Charged with functionalism. Institutions in his account fit their purpose a little too
  neatly for something nobody designed.
</Objections>

## Where to go next

- Deleuze, "Postscript on the Societies of Control" (1990). Six pages arguing that the
  enclosures Foucault described were already dissolving as he wrote. Read it
  immediately after *Discipline and Punish*, not months later. It is in
  [control after discipline](/milieus/control-after-discipline).
- Goffman, *Asylums* (1961), the microphysics that pairs with this and is much easier
  to test against your own experience of any institution.
- [Illich](/thinkers/illich), who reaches a similar conclusion about institutions from
  a completely different direction and is more use if you want to build something.
