Agamben is the synthesizer. He takes [Schmitt's](/thinkers/schmitt) sovereign who
decides the exception, fuses it with [Foucault's](/thinkers/foucault) biopolitics, adds
Walter Benjamin and Hannah Arendt, and produces a single claim about the structure of
Western politics.

His own room matters here. He was writing after the Italian *anni di piombo*, the years
of lead, when the Italian state governed political terrorism through emergency
legislation and preventive detention. Like Schmitt, he is generalizing from one
country's experience of emergency government, and that should be said rather than
hidden.

<Thesis source="State of Exception, 2003">
In modern politics the exception has become the rule.
</Thesis>

## Homo sacer, and why the figure is strange

Agamben recovers an obscure figure from archaic Roman law.
<Term id="homo-sacer">Homo sacer</Term>, the sacred man, is defined by a paradox: he may
be killed but he may not be sacrificed. Anyone can kill him without it counting as
murder, because he stands outside human law. He cannot be used in religious sacrifice,
because he stands outside divine law. Doubly excluded, and that exclusion is exactly
how he is held inside the order.

The structural claim that follows: sovereign and homo sacer are two ends of one
apparatus. The sovereign is the one for whom all people are potentially homo sacer. Homo
sacer is the one in relation to whom everyone acts as sovereign.

<Term id="bare-life">Bare life</Term> rests on a distinction Agamben takes from Greek.
*Zoē* is bare biological life, the fact of living, shared with animals. *Bios* is a form
of life, shaped politically and ethically, proper to a person in a community. His
radicalization of Foucault is that Western politics is founded from the beginning on the
capture of *zoē*: the first sovereign act is to seize bare life and draw it into
politics as the thing politics decides over.

<WorkedExample label="Worked example, and the reason this page exists in this form" title="Agamben on COVID, or what happens when a framework is applied without discipline">
During the pandemic Agamben published a series of interventions reading public-health
measures as the naked face of the permanent state of exception, and equating quarantine
and distancing with the logic of the camp. They were widely condemned, including by
people who had spent careers defending his work.

The criticism was not that he was rude about lockdowns. It was that the apparatus had
stopped discriminating. A framework that cannot tell the difference between a
contestable public-health measure and totalitarian capture is not detecting the
exception any more. It is detecting itself. Once you own that hammer, every event
arrives shaped like the exception.

This site publishes the episode as a featured example rather than burying it, because it
is the cleanest available demonstration of the second failure mode described on the
[method page](/method): a powerful critique curdling into a theory of everything. The
strong use of these tools keeps the lens and keeps the discipline to ask, each time,
whether the lens fits this case.

The discipline is testable. Ask what evidence would have made the framework wrong here.
If nothing would have, the framework was not being used. It was being recited.
</WorkedExample>

## Primary works

- *Homo Sacer* (1995).
- *State of Exception* (2003). The most useful single volume if you read one.
- *Remnants of Auschwitz* (1998).
- *What Is an Apparatus?* (2006). Short.

<Objections>
- Historically and legally imprecise. Legal scholars argue that he collapses
  constitutionally regulated emergency powers together with lawless rule, and flattens
  enormous differences between detention regimes into one figure.
- The claim that the camp is the paradigm of modernity risks trivializing the Holocaust
  by generalizing it, and that objection comes from historians of the period as well as
  from philosophers.
- Politically it tends toward quietism. If everything already is the camp, ordinary legal
  and political remedies look pointless, and the COVID episode above is what that looks
  like in practice.
</Objections>

## Where to go next

- Walter Benjamin, *Critique of Violence* (1921), on law-making violence as distinct
  from law-preserving violence. This is the source of Agamben's dialogue with Schmitt.
- Hannah Arendt, *The Origins of Totalitarianism*, on statelessness and the right to
  have rights, which is the same problem approached with evidence.
- Judith Butler, *Precarious Life*, as a counter-model that keeps the concern and drops
  the totalizing structure.
- Achille Mbembe, "Necropolitics" (2003), for the same question asked from the colony,
  in [control after discipline](/milieus/control-after-discipline).
