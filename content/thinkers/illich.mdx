Illich was a Catholic priest who ran a parish in New York, became vice-rector of the
Catholic University of Puerto Rico, and in 1961 founded a centre in Cuernavaca, Mexico,
that trained missionaries while he argued that the missionary and development apparatus
should not exist. He funded it with language-course fees rather than foundation grants,
which is why he could write what he wrote. The Vatican investigated him in 1968.

<Thesis source="Deschooling Society, 1971, and Tools for Conviviality, 1973">
Past a threshold of scale, an institution produces the opposite of its promise and
disables the people it serves.
</Thesis>

<Mechanism />

## What school actually teaches

School installs a set of confusions we then mistake for reality: teaching with
learning, grades with education, attendance with knowledge, diplomas with competence.

The damage is not in the syllabus. It is in the
<Term id="hidden-curriculum">hidden curriculum</Term>, the lesson taught by the form of
the thing regardless of content: that life consists of consuming institutionally
packaged services, that you cannot do important things yourself, that process is the
same as substance. Its deepest teaching is dependence on institutions. It produces
people who, facing any need at all, look for the accredited provider.

## Radical monopoly, the most portable idea here

An ordinary monopoly is one company cornering a market. A
<Term id="radical-monopoly">radical monopoly</Term> is one *way* of meeting a need
dominating so completely that the other ways stop being possible.

The car is the worked example, above. Schooling is the other: once it holds the
monopoly, learning that happens outside it does not count, and a self-taught person's
knowledge is invisible for want of a credential.

It is radical because it works at the root, on the need itself, closing off
alternatives before anyone can conceive of them. This is a sharper question than most
current antitrust language, which asks whether a firm has excluded competitors rather
than whether a mode of provision has abolished the alternatives. Applying it properly
to platforms is one of the [open questions](/open-questions).

## Counterproductivity, with cases

- *Medicine.* <Term id="iatrogenesis">Iatrogenesis</Term> is sickness caused by
  medicine: clinical (drug effects, hospital infection), social (the medicalization of
  ordinary life and the expanding definition of illness), and cultural, the deepest,
  where the system takes away our capacity to face our own pain and death without
  management.
- *Transport.* Past a threshold it produces immobility and consumes the time it saves.
  *Energy and Equity* (1974) contains the calculation, and it is worth doing yourself
  with your own commute.
- *School.* Past a threshold it produces ignorance and learned helplessness.

## Conviviality, which is the constructive half

A <Term id="convivial-tool">convivial tool</Term> is one anyone can pick up, when they
like, for their own purposes, and which makes them more capable: the bicycle, the
library, the telephone, hand tools. A manipulative tool enforces dependence, requires a
certified priesthood to operate, dictates what the user does, and reshapes the world so
that you have to come to it.

Illich is the only figure here who is mainly interested in building rather than
diagnosing. Anything constructed out of this material should treat
*Tools for Conviviality* as its design brief.

## Psychological underpinning

Learned helplessness, from Martin Seligman, is the experimental form of the disabling
professions argument. Alongside it, deskilling and the atrophy of self-efficacy, and
the way institutional framing gets into how a need is experienced in the first place.

## Primary works

- *Deschooling Society* (1971).
- *Tools for Conviviality* (1973).
- *Energy and Equity* (1974).
- *Medical Nemesis*, published in Britain as *Limits to Medicine* (1975).
- *Disabling Professions* (1977).

<Objections>
- The alternatives are thin. Learning webs were sketched and never built at scale, and
  it is not clear what replaces mass schooling without making inequality worse.
- Anti-institutionalism cuts both ways. Dismantling public provision hands the ground to
  markets, and parts of Illich were taken up enthusiastically by deregulation advocates
  he would have opposed.
- The medical critique, taken literally, has been used to justify refusing treatment
  that works.
- An intellectually independent polymath underestimates how much institutional
  scaffolding most people actually need.
</Objections>
