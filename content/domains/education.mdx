Start with the claim you have probably already met online, because it is the most
mangled one in this whole field, and the honest version is more interesting than the
viral version.

## The defensible version

The nineteenth-century Prussian system was explicitly two-track.

- The *Gymnasium*: humanistic, autonomy-forming, built on the Humboldtian idea of
  *Bildung*, meaning the cultivation of a whole person. For the administrative and
  professional elite.
- The *Volksschule*: an elementary mass track oriented toward literacy, punctuality,
  obedience and readiness for industry and conscription.

Compulsory attendance, age-graded cohorts, standardized curricula, teacher certification
and the bell-timed day were Prussian administrative innovations. Horace Mann visited in
1843, imported the mass-schooling logic to Massachusetts, and it spread through the
United States from there. Japan's Meiji reformers and much of Latin America drew on the
same template.

## The version to avoid

That a specific group of people met and designed schooling to produce obedient factory
workers, and said so.

The honest formulation is structural. A state facing conscription needs, industrial
labour demand and the problem of governing a mass population built an institution whose
form solved those problems. The form then outlived the original purpose and now
reproduces itself for reasons nobody currently chooses. That is a stronger claim,
because it survives contact with evidence, and it is harder to dismiss.

## What the research actually shows

**Philip Jackson, *Life in Classrooms* (1968)** coined the
<Term id="hidden-curriculum">hidden curriculum</Term>: the lessons taught by school's
form rather than its syllabus. He summarized them as crowds, praise and power. Learn to
wait. Learn to be evaluated. Learn to accept unequal authority.

**Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis, *Schooling in Capitalist America* (1976)** proposed
the <Term id="correspondence-principle">correspondence principle</Term>: the social
relations of school mirror the social relations of the workplace, so school reproduces
the workforce structurally rather than through anything in the lessons. It was
criticized as economically determinist and for underweighting student resistance, and
the authors themselves later revised the argument.

**Jean Anyon, "Social Class and the Hidden Curriculum of Work" (1980)** is the single
most usable study here for a general reader. She did fieldwork in schools serving
different class communities and found they taught different relationships to knowledge
and authority: rote procedure in working-class schools, creativity and negotiation in
affluent professional schools, analytic reasoning and rule-making in elite schools. Same
country, same decade, same official curriculum.

<Contested status="Provocation, not a source">
**John Taylor Gatto**, *The Underground History of American Education* and *Dumbing Us
Down*, is the popular entry point and the reason many people arrive at this subject. His
rhetorical force is real. His historical sourcing is loose and several claims do not
survive checking. Use him as a list of things to verify, not as evidence. Saying this
openly costs nothing and buys the rest of the argument credibility.
</Contested>

## What the school does to a person who resists it

**Paul Willis, *Learning to Labour* (1977)** is the necessary complication and the best
ethnography in this domain. The working-class boys he followed were not fooled by school.
They saw through it completely, treated its promises as a con, and built a culture of
resistance around that insight.

Then they left at sixteen for factory work, which is exactly where the system needed them.
Seeing through an institution is not the same as escaping it, and the resistance itself
delivered them.

Read it next to **Annette Lareau, *Unequal Childhoods* (2003)**, the American fieldwork on
concerted cultivation against natural growth, and **Basil Bernstein** on elaborated and
restricted linguistic codes, which is where the mechanism gets specific about language.

## The constructive answer

Paulo Freire, *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* (1968), teaches literacy and the reading of one's
own situation as the same operation, and Everett Reimer's *School Is Dead* runs alongside
Illich. Both are on the [counter-tradition](/counter-tradition) page, which exists so that
this domain does not end in a shrug.

## Who carries the theory

[Bourdieu](/thinkers/bourdieu) on how the school converts inherited advantage into
certified merit, and [Illich](/thinkers/illich) on what the institution teaches by
existing. [Foucault](/thinkers/foucault) supplies the timetable and the examination.

Lewis Mumford's argument about the monastery clock, in
[media materialism](/milieus/media-materialism), is the deeper version of the timetable
claim, and it predates the factory by centuries.
