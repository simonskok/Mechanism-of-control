Reading about how influence works does very little. Being able to test a specific thing
in front of you does a lot more, and it is a different kind of knowledge: a skill rather
than a conclusion.

Both tools below are already used elsewhere on this site. They are collected here because
a tool described in passing is a fact, and a tool set out on its own is something you can
pick up.

Neither one tells you what to think about anything. They tell you what to check.

## The four questions

Put these to any source: a study, a report, a documentary, a think tank paper, a news
article, a course syllabus. They are the questions this whole site is built on, and they
are on the [method page](/method) for that reason.

1. **Who paid, and what did they want?**
   Not who wrote it. Who funded the work, and what would count as a useful result for
   them. Funding shapes findings through selection far more often than through
   instruction. Nobody has to be told what to conclude.

2. **What was the institution's actual job?**
   Not its mission statement. What it exists to do, day to day, and what it is measured
   on. [A bureau measuring radio audiences for advertisers](/milieus/radio-project)
   produces a different theory of the public than a seminar writing against fascism, even
   when the two share a building.

3. **What crisis was in the room?**
   What was going badly at the time this was made. Ideas about control get written when
   control is visibly failing or visibly being built, and the crisis usually explains the
   shape of the argument better than the author's biography does.

4. **What could not be said there?**
   What would have ended a career inside that institution. The boundary of the sayable
   is often the most informative thing about a place, and it never appears in the
   document itself.

A worked answer looks like this. The
[Congress for Cultural Freedom](/milieus/congress-for-cultural-freedom) funded good
magazines that published honest writers, and most of those writers did not know the money
came from the CIA. Nobody lied. The four questions still return something: the money set
which topics had a well paid venue, which journals could afford translation, and which
careers became possible. That is what patronage does instead of censorship.

Run the questions on this site too. The answers are on the
[reflexive page](/reflexive), including the ones that are awkward.

## Eight signs of coercive persuasion

Robert Jay Lifton set these out in 1961, after interviewing people who had been through
thought reform programmes in China. They were written about an extreme case and they work
on ordinary ones, which is why they have outlasted their original subject.

The point is not that a group showing one of these is sinister. Most institutions show
one or two. The point is the count, and the direction of travel.

1. **Milieu control.** How much of what you hear comes through one channel. Who decides
   what reaches you, and whether you have contact with people outside it.

2. **Mystical manipulation.** Events arranged to look spontaneous, presented as proof of
   the group's rightness. Things that were planned, experienced as things that happened.

3. **The demand for purity.** The world split into pure and impure with nothing between.
   Since nobody can reach the standard, guilt becomes permanent and useful.

4. **Confession.** Personal failings disclosed and recorded. The disclosure is framed as
   relief and functions as leverage.

5. **Sacred science.** A doctrine held to be both scientifically true and morally
   absolute, so that questioning it is both an error and a sin. This one is easy to miss
   because it can wear the language of evidence.

6. **Loading the language.** Thought-terminating clichés: short phrases that end an
   argument rather than answer it. The vocabulary shrinks, and questions that cannot be
   asked in the available words stop being asked. This is the single most useful item on
   the list.

7. **Doctrine over person.** When your own experience contradicts the doctrine, your
   experience is what gets corrected.

8. **The dispensing of existence.** The group decides who counts as a real person.
   Outsiders are a lower category, and leaving means ceasing to exist in a way that
   matters.

Lifton's own warning applies to the list itself: it describes a direction, not a verdict.
Used as a checklist for labelling people, it becomes the thing it describes.

## How to use either one

Run them on something you agree with first. Both tools are easy to point at opponents and
that is the use with the least value, because it returns the answer you already had.

Pointed at your own sources they return something you did not know, which is the only
reason to have a tool at all.
