[Havel's](/figures/havel) greengrocer puts a sign in his window. He does not believe it, nobody
thinks he does, and nobody reads the words.

The usual reading is that the sign shows how total the ideology was. Havel's reading is the
opposite, and it is the more useful one. The sign is necessary **because** the ideology is
dead. A living belief does not need to be displayed on demand. Nobody has to be required to
perform something they actually hold.

That inversion is the whole of this page. **Ritual expands as belief contracts.** A rise in
required performances is evidence that persuasion has failed and been replaced by something
cheaper.

## What Havel actually described

He was careful about the mechanism, and it is worth following.

The sign's stated content is irrelevant. Its real message is: I am obedient, and therefore I
have the right to be left alone. Havel points out that if the placard read *I am afraid and
therefore unquestioningly obedient*, the greengrocer would be ashamed to display it. The
ideological wording exists to prevent exactly that shame. It lets a person perform submission
while describing it to themselves as principle.

Second, the performance is not addressed upward to the authorities. It is addressed sideways,
to everyone else. Each person's compliance is part of the pressure on the next person. This is
why Havel says there is no clean line between victims and enforcers. The greengrocer is both,
and so is the neighbour who sees his sign and puts up their own.

Third, and this is the part people skip: Havel argued the system had become autonomous.
Nobody was directing it. The officials performed too. The ritual had detached from anyone's
belief, including the rulers', and continued under its own momentum.

## Havel's own warning about applying this

He anticipated the move this page is about to make, and he did not think the West should read
his essay as being about somewhere else. He described the post-totalitarian system as a mirror
held up to modern civilization generally, and argued that the consumer democracies were
running a related process by different means: people adapting to a system that hollows out
what they are, and calling the adaptation freedom.

That is permission to generalize. It is not permission to equate. The
[objections](/figures/havel) on Havel's own page state the limit plainly: in the system he
described, refusing the ritual cost you your job, your children's education and sometimes your
liberty. Almost nowhere that people now invoke the greengrocer carries that price, and
dropping the price is how the comparison becomes self-flattering.

## A test you can run

The diagnostic is not what a ritual says. It is whether the saying is doing any work. Four
questions, and they are only useful if you run them on your own side first.

**Would anyone notice if it were false?** A statement nobody checks is not a claim. It is a
signal.

**Is refusal punished out of proportion to the content?** If declining to say something costs
far more than the something is worth, the words were never the point. The compliance was.

**Is the real message "I am compliant" rather than the literal content?** Havel's whole
argument in one question.

**Has the performance replaced the thing it refers to?** This is
<Term id="goodharts-law">Goodhart's law</Term> arriving in a new place. When the report
substitutes for the safety, the training for the change, the statement for the practice, you
are looking at ritual.

Candidates across every part of contemporary life: corporate values displayed on office walls,
mandatory trainings that change nothing and are known to change nothing, compliance
documentation produced for audit, pledges and loyalty displays of every political colour,
professional codes nobody consults, statements of commitment required before unrelated work,
safety theatre, and the performance of position on social platforms where the audience is
sideways rather than upward, exactly as Havel described.

The list is deliberately not sorted by politics. A reader who can only find examples among
people they already dislike has not run the test, they have used it as a weapon, and that is
the failure this site names as [superiority](/reflexive).

## The sacred does not disappear, it relocates

The broader claim, that ritual and the sacred reappear wherever formal religion recedes, is
older than any of this and belongs to Durkheim. His argument was that the sacred is a social
fact rather than a supernatural one: communities generate a division between sacred and
profane, mark it with ritual, and renew their cohesion through collective assembly. On that
account a society does not stop producing sacred objects and heresies when it stops attending
church. It produces them elsewhere.

Robert Bellah extended this to civil religion, the observation that political life carries its
own sacred texts, founding myths, martyrs and rites, and that these do work no ordinary
policy language can do.

This is why the argument here is not that something new and strange is happening. It is that a
very old mechanism is operating in places that do not describe themselves as religious, and
that the people inside it experience their rituals as reasoning, exactly as everyone always
has.

Two cautions, and they matter. Calling something a religion is a rhetorical move with a long
history of being used to dismiss opponents without engaging them, and it should be resisted
for that reason alone. And the Durkheimian claim is a functional one about what ritual does
for a group. It is not evidence that any particular contemporary movement is false, since a
belief can be both true and ritually enforced, and the ritual test says nothing about the
content.

## Why this is good news rather than bad

If ritual expands as belief contracts, then the visible growth of required performances is not
a sign that something is winning. It is a sign that it has stopped being able to persuade and
has fallen back on compliance.

That is the same finding as [what control is evidence of](/what-control-reveals), arriving
through a different door. [Gramsci](/thinkers/gramsci) said coercion becomes visible when
consent fails. Havel said ritual becomes compulsory when belief dies. Both are describing a
substitution, and a substitution is a weaker arrangement than the thing it replaced.

It also means Havel's counter-move is available and cheap in most of the places a reader will
encounter this. Living in truth is not a programme and it overthrows nothing. It is declining
to perform, and it works because it removes your contribution to the pressure on the person
next to you.

Whether that is safe to do depends entirely on what it costs where you are, and that is a
question only you can answer honestly.
