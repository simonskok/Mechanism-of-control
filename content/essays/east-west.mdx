The phrase East and West carries two different arguments in this material, and blurring
them wastes both.

## First, Gramsci's technical distinction

[Gramsci](/thinkers/gramsci) used East and West to describe how thick civil society is,
and he drew a strategic conclusion from it.

**In the East**, meaning Tsarist Russia, the state was everything and civil society was
thin. A frontal assault could take power in a single blow. October 1917 worked because
there was very little between the state and the population to absorb the shock.

**In the West**, civil society is thick: schools, press, churches, unions, associations,
a whole system of fortifications standing behind the state. Frontal assault fails there,
and Gramsci had watched it fail across postwar Europe. What is required instead is a long
<Term id="war-of-position">war of position</Term> inside civil society, to win
intellectual ground and build a rival worldview, as the precondition of political power.

You have to win the culture before you can win the state. The best empirical test of that
claim is [the neoliberal thought collective](/milieus/mont-pelerin), which ran exactly
that strategy for fifty years, from the right, and won.

## Second, the controlled experiment

The twentieth century ran something close to an experiment. Two regimes pursuing social
control by opposite primary means, on the same continent, at the same time. Each is best
understood through the other.

| | Eastern bloc, state socialist | Western liberal capitalist |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary instrument** | Coercion, with consent secondary | Consent, with coercion in reserve |
| **Where truth is managed** | Central censorship. A visible office decides what may be printed | Market and institutional filtering. No office, but ownership, advertising dependence, sourcing routines and career incentives shape what gets said |
| **Relation to ideology** | Explicit, official, compulsory, widely disbelieved | Implicit, unofficial, optional, widely believed |
| **Characteristic subject** | The dissembler, who says the required words, believes none of them, and knows others do not either | The sincere participant, who is required to say nothing and genuinely holds the ambient worldview |
| **Failure mode** | Legitimacy collapse. Everyone knows, the system rots, and it falls fast | Legitimacy without alternatives. No collapse point, because dissent is absorbed, marketed and monetized |
| **How dissent ends** | Suppression, prison, exile | Absorption. Made into a niche, a genre, a product, a career |

The uncomfortable conclusion, and the reason this comparison earns its place: overt
censorship produces a population fluent in reading between the lines. A consent-based
order produces a population with no practice at it, because nothing appears to be hidden.

[The Congress for Cultural Freedom](/milieus/congress-for-cultural-freedom) is the bridge
between the two columns. It is the West doing censorship's job through patronage, and
arriving at a comparable outcome by the other method.

## Havel's greengrocer

**Václav Havel, *The Power of the Powerless* (1978)** is the single best text on the
Eastern side, and if you read one thing from this site's [reading paths](/reading), read
this.

A greengrocer places a sign in his window among the onions and carrots: "Workers of the
world, unite!" He does not believe it. Nobody thinks he believes it. Nobody reads it.

So what is the sign for? Its real message is: I am obedient, and therefore I have the
right to be left alone. It is a signal of compliance addressed to the people who check.

Havel calls the system <Term id="post-totalitarianism">post-totalitarian</Term> because
it no longer needs anyone to believe anything. It runs on
<Term id="living-within-a-lie">living within a lie</Term>: each person's ritual
compliance becomes part of the pressure holding the next person in place. Everyone is
simultaneously victim and enforcer, and there is no one to blame at the centre because
the centre is distributed through everybody's front window.

The counter-move is living in truth: taking the sign down. It is dangerous precisely
because it is not a political programme. It shows the lie is a lie, and the people still
displaying signs know it.

Read this next to [Gramsci](/thinkers/gramsci) and [Bourdieu](/thinkers/bourdieu). Havel
describes hegemony without belief, which is the limit case both theories have to survive.
Read it next to Festinger too, in [psychology](/domains/psychology): having acted, we
revise belief to match the act, which is the laboratory version of the greengrocer.

## The rest of the canon, largely missing from Western syllabi

- **Czesław Miłosz, *The Captive Mind* (1953).** The psychology of intellectual
  accommodation under an imposed ideology. He borrows the Persian term
  <Term id="ketman">ketman</Term> for the art of professing the required doctrine while
  keeping a private reservation, and shows how the self-division eventually consumes the
  self it was meant to protect. The finest psychological study of complicity in this
  literature.
- **György Konrád and Iván Szelényi, *The Intellectuals on the Road to Class Power*
  (1979).** Written in Hungary. Under state socialism the intelligentsia was itself
  becoming a ruling class, through a monopoly over planning knowledge. It is a structural
  critique of expert rule from inside the East, and it arrives at James Burnham's
  managerial-revolution conclusion from the opposite side of the Iron Curtain. That
  convergence deserves more attention than it gets.
- **István Bibó**, on the political culture of small Eastern European states.
  Indispensable for the regional frame.
- **Alexander Zinoviev, *Homo Sovieticus* (1982).** Satirical anthropology of the
  character type the system produced.
- **Tony Judt, *Postwar* (2005).** The reliable single-volume history underneath all of
  it.
- **Ivan Krastev and Stephen Holmes, *The Light That Failed* (2019).** The post-1989
  synthesis: the politics of imitation, what it does to a society to be told its task is
  to become a copy of somewhere else, and how resentment of that shaped the region's
  politics afterwards. The most direct link between this page and current events.

## What each side reveals about the other

The East demonstrates that **control does not require belief**. Ritual compliance is
sufficient, and may be more stable than sincerity, until suddenly it is not.

The West demonstrates that **control does not require an office**. Dispersed incentives
produce coordinated outcomes with no coordinator, which is exactly
[Foucault's](/thinkers/foucault) capillary power and the propaganda model's filters.

Together they refute the two lazy readings. Control requires a conspiracy: no, the East
had an actual apparatus and still lost the population's belief. The absence of a
conspiracy means the absence of control: no, the West has no apparatus and holds belief
far more securely.
