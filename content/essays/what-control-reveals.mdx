Most of this site describes how control works. This page is about what its presence tells you,
which is a different question and a more useful one.

The claim is simple. **Control is what gets installed where something else stopped working.**
Surveillance is what you build when you cannot rely on trust. Metrics are what you install
when you cannot rely on judgement. Ritual is what remains when belief has gone. Read that way,
a control system is not a display of strength. It is a record of a failure, and it usually
names the failure precisely.

This is not a claim about the psychology of the people who build these systems. Nothing on
this site supports the idea that they were damaged or compensating, and the
[method](/method) here is to look at institutions rather than to diagnose individuals. It is a
claim about what conditions produce control, and four of the thinkers already on this site
arrive at it from different directions.

## Four routes to the same finding

**[Gramsci](/thinkers/gramsci).** Rule works through <Term id="hegemony">hegemony</Term>, which
is consent, and coercion sits in reserve. The implication runs backwards: when force becomes
visible, consent has failed. Police in the street are not the system working. They are the
system having run out of the cheaper instrument. He called the moment an
<Term id="organic-crisis">organic crisis</Term>.

**[Havel](/figures/havel).** The greengrocer's sign is required precisely because nobody
believes the thing it says. <Term id="post-totalitarianism">Post-totalitarianism</Term> runs on
ritual because it no longer has faith to run on. The ritual is not evidence of the ideology's
strength. It is the marker on its grave. This has a present-day reading of its own, on
[ritual without belief](/ritual).

**[Michael Mann](/milieus/infrastructural-power).**
<Term id="despotic-power">Despotic power</Term>, what elites can do without negotiating, has
fallen in modern states. <Term id="infrastructural-power">Infrastructural power</Term>, the
capacity to reach into society and actually implement, has risen to an unprecedented level.
States reach further into ordinary life as their ability to simply command it declines.

**[Goodhart](/domains/bureaucracy).** You measure when you cannot trust judgement. Every target
regime is a statement that the institution no longer believes its own people will do the work
without being counted, and Goodhart's law then guarantees the measure stops measuring.

Four fields, one shape. Control expands where something more reliable has been lost.

## The same finding about people

The structural claim has an individual-level counterpart, and it is better evidenced than most
of what gets said about power and personality.

[David McClelland](/figures/mcclelland) split the drive for influence in two. Wanting power for
its own sake, treated as something won at another's expense, comes with little self-control and
correlates with impulsive, exploitative and group-damaging decisions. Wanting influence in
order to accomplish something for a group involves the same drive with restraint on top of it.
The variable separating them is <Term id="activity-inhibition">activity inhibition</Term>, a
disposition to regulate your own impulses.

[Adler](/figures/adler) reached the same distinction sixty years earlier without experiments.
Striving over other people never finishes, because it is measured against persons and there is
always someone else. Striving toward a task can be satisfied, because it is measured against
the work.

[Fromm](/figures/fromm) states the mechanism most bluntly: the need for absolute power over
another person is what appears when the capacity to produce, relate or create has failed.

None of this licenses the conclusion that powerful people are secretly weak. It is not a
diagnosis and it cannot be run on individuals from a distance. What it supports is narrower
and more useful: the drive to control others and the capacity to govern yourself are different
things, and there is evidence they trade off.

## What follows, which is not what people expect

**Order does not require control.** [Tom Tyler](/figures/tyler) has spent decades on why people
obey, and the finding is consistent: <Term id="procedural-justice">procedural justice</Term>,
meaning the perceived fairness of the process, predicts compliance better than the probability
of getting caught. People accept decisions that go against them when they had a voice, when
the process was the same for everyone, when they were treated as a person, and when they could
read the decision-maker's motives.

That makes humane correction the effective option rather than the soft one, and it gives you a
test for any institution you deal with. An institution failing those four is relying on threat,
which by Tyler's evidence is the weaker instrument.

**The capacity is trainable.** This matters more than anything else on the page. Activity
inhibition is a disposition rather than a fixed trait. [Bandura's](/glossary#self-efficacy)
<Term id="self-efficacy">self-efficacy</Term> is built through doing hard things and
succeeding. The 2016 reformulation of learned helplessness by Maier and Seligman reversed the
original claim: passivity under prolonged stress is the default response, and what is actually
learned is control.

So the variable this site has been describing for fifty pages, the presence or absence of
self-restraint and trustworthiness in an institution, is the one variable a reader has direct
access to in themselves.

## The part that is not consoling

Two warnings, because this argument is easy to turn into flattery and flattery is the failure
mode this site is most exposed to.

It does not mean the people running things are pathetic and you are healthy. That reading is
unfalsifiable, it contradicts the method, and it is the [superiority](/reflexive) problem with a
psychological vocabulary attached.

And it does not mean control is fragile. A system built on failed trust can last for
generations and do enormous damage on the way. Reading a control system as a confession tells
you what went wrong and where. It does not tell you the thing is about to fall over, and
[Havel](/figures/havel) waited twenty years.

What it does is change what you are looking at. Not an all-powerful arrangement that was always
going to be this way, but a specific set of substitutions made at specific times because
something more reliable was unavailable. Substitutions can be identified. Some of them can be
reversed. And the capacity they replaced is one you can build.
