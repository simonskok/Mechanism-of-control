There is a reasonable expectation about a site like this one: that it hands over facts, and
that a reader who has them is better off. That expectation is mostly wrong, and the research
on why is old and fairly settled.

This page is about what actually happens to a person who takes this material in. It is here
because a site that describes mechanisms of control and ignores what describing them does to
a reader has not understood its own subject.

## The information is not the problem

[Jack Mezirow](/figures/mezirow) spent his career on adults who went back into education and
found that most learning changes nothing. As long as an experience fits the way you already
make sense of things, you file it and carry on. Change starts with what he called the
disorienting dilemma: something that will not fit, and that makes the frame itself look wrong.

Almost everything on this site is aimed at producing one. That school sorted you before it
taught you. That the assessment never ends. That the institution promising to help you
produces the opposite. None of these are complicated facts. They are simple facts that will
not sit inside the usual frame.

[Ronnie Janoff-Bulman](/figures/janoff-bulman) explains why that feels disproportionate to the
content. What breaks in a shock is rarely a belief. It is the unstated assumptions underneath,
that the world is broadly benign and broadly makes sense. Her work is about trauma and this
site is not traumatic, but the shape is the same, and it is why people describe this material
as disorienting rather than as interesting.

[Robert Kegan](/figures/kegan) gives the mechanism. What you are embedded in you cannot see,
because you look through it rather than at it. Growth is the conversion of that into something
you can hold and examine. Your <Term id="habitus">habitus</Term> was subject and felt like
personality. The point of these pages is to make it object. Kegan is clear that this involves
loss before it involves gain, since what you could not see was also what you were.

## The four things that go wrong

[William Perry](/figures/perry) studied undergraduates in the 1950s and 1960s and found that
discovering knowledge is contested does not reliably make people wiser. He documented
**retreat**, a return to certainty with fresh hard answers and hostility toward whatever
caused the doubt, and **escape**, a drift into detachment where everything is a perspective so
nothing requires commitment. Those are the two ditches on either side of this material, found
in students seventy years ago.

Three more, all documented, all specifically likely here.

**Moral licensing.** Establishing that you are one of the good ones licenses doing less
afterwards rather than more. Understanding a problem can settle the account. A reader who
finishes this site feeling they have dealt with something has in fact only read about it, and
the feeling is the risk.

**Psychic numbing.** Response does not scale with the size of a harm, it fades, and the fading
begins as early as the second victim. More documented cases of control buys coverage at the
cost of response, which is a direct argument against making this site bigger.

**The illusion of explanatory depth.** People believe they understand a mechanism far better
than they do, and the confidence collapses the moment they have to explain it step by step.
Reading fluently feels like knowing. It is not the same thing, and the gap is invisible from
inside.

That last one is why the mechanism stepper on every thinker page asks you to explain the step
before it shows you. Nothing you write is saved or sent anywhere. The point is the attempt,
because the attempt is what reveals whether the understanding is there.

## The hardest objection

[Peter Sloterdijk](/figures/sloterdijk) argues the assumption underneath this entire site
stopped being true. The Enlightenment expected that exposing an illusion would break it.
Instead we produced people who see the mechanism clearly, describe it accurately, and change
nothing. He calls it enlightened false consciousness, and treats it as critique fully absorbed
and rendered harmless.

If he is right, agreement is worthless. A reader who finishes and nods has cost the
arrangement nothing.

One thing that is not the danger, contrary to a widely repeated claim: people digging in when
corrected. The backfire effect, reported in 2010, has largely failed to replicate. The biggest
attempt, by Wood and Porter in 2019 across five experiments and more than ten thousand
participants, using items chosen as most likely to produce it, found that backfire is
stubbornly difficult to induce and that people generally accept corrections even about
political allies.

So the risk is not rejection. It is frictionless agreement.

## What actually helps

Mezirow's fourth phase is recognizing that the discontent is shared. In his model that is not
encouragement, it is structural: a person who believes their dislocation is personal has no
route from self-examination into anything else, and usually converts the whole thing into
private shame.

That is the strongest argument available that this site cannot only supply information. The
evidence that other people carry the same concern does work that no further explanation does.

Perry's endpoint is the same shape. He called it commitment within relativism: choosing a
position and living by it while knowing it is contestable. His finding is that it is not
reached by knowing more. It is reached by having to act on a position while holding its
uncertainty, which is an argument for having something to do rather than something to finish.

Which is why the [tools](/tools) page exists, and why
[what control is evidence of](/what-control-reveals) is on this site at all.

## A note on this page

The psychology here is contested in places and some of it sits near the replication crisis.
Self-affirmation effects are smaller and more moderated than early work claimed. Mezirow's
phases are hard to falsify. Perry's sample was Harvard. Janoff-Bulman's assumptions may not be
universal. Each figure page carries its own objections and they are worth reading before
relying on any of this.

The honest position is that these are design heuristics with evidence behind them rather than
established facts, and that nobody has tested any of them on this particular material.
