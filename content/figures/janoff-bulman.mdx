Janoff-Bulman explains why some information does not behave like information.

<Thesis source="Shattered Assumptions, 1992">
People run on three assumptions they never state: the world is benevolent, the world is
meaningful, and the self is worthy. Trauma does not add a fact. It breaks those.
</Thesis>

## The assumptive world

The term <Term id="assumptive-world">assumptive world</Term> comes from Colin Murray Parkes,
working on bereavement, and describes the background model of reality a person operates from
without ever examining it. Janoff-Bulman's contribution was to specify its contents and study
what happens when it fails.

Her three core assumptions are that the world is broadly benevolent, that events make sense
and are distributed in some comprehensible way, and that the self is decent and worth
something. Ordinary life confirms them constantly and invisibly.

When something arrives that cannot coexist with them, the result is not a revised belief. It
is the temporary loss of the frame that beliefs were held in, which is why people describe it
as unreality rather than as bad news.

## Rebuilding, not restoring

Her account of recovery is the useful part. The old assumptive world does not come back. What
happens, when it goes well, is the construction of a new one that can accommodate what
happened, usually less benign than the first and more durable.

## Why a site about control needs this

Most of what is on these pages is mild by her standards. Nobody is traumatized by reading
about credentialism. But the shape is the same: the material does not add facts so much as
challenge the frame the facts were in, particularly the assumption that the arrangement you
are inside is broadly benign and broadly makes sense.

That is why the experience of reading it is disproportionate to its content, and why the
useful response is not more information but time and something to rebuild with.

<Objections>
- The three assumptions were derived from work with trauma survivors, and it is contested
  whether they are universal or specific to the populations and cultures studied.
- Much of the evidence is retrospective self-report, which is vulnerable to people
  reconstructing what they used to believe.
- The related idea of post-traumatic growth, which grew from the same area, has been
  criticized because reported growth often does not match measured change over time.
- Applying a trauma framework to ordinary intellectual discomfort risks inflating both, and
  this page should be read as a structural analogy rather than a clinical claim.
</Objections>
