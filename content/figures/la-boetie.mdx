Written around 1548, when he was about eighteen, and circulated in manuscript rather than
printed. It is the oldest text in this project and it asks the question everything else here
is still answering.

<Thesis source="Discourse on Voluntary Servitude, c. 1548">
A tyrant has no power except the power people lend him. Stop lending it and he falls, with
no violence required.
</Thesis>

## The argument

A tyrant is one man. He has two eyes, two hands, one body. Everything he uses against you,
he got from you: the soldiers are your sons, the money is your taxes, the informers are your
neighbours. He is strong only in the sense that you carry him.

So the puzzle is not oppression. It is obedience. Why do people who could simply stop, not
stop?

His answers are still the ones in use. Habit, first: people born into a condition take it for
nature and cannot imagine the alternative. Then the ladder of complicity, which is the sharp
part: the tyrant buys a handful of people, those buy a few hundred, the hundreds buy
thousands, until a large part of the population has a stake in the arrangement and polices it
without being told to.

## Why it belongs here

Every later argument on this site is a version of this one with more evidence.
[Gramsci](/thinkers/gramsci) supplies the institutions that manufacture the habit.
[Bourdieu](/thinkers/bourdieu) supplies the school that installs it in a child.
[Havel](/figures/havel) supplies the greengrocer, which is the ladder of complicity described
from inside a single shop window.

<Objections>
- It is a moral argument rather than an empirical one. Withdrawing consent is easy to write
  and lethal to attempt, and the essay has little to say about the cost.
- It treats obedience as a choice people could simply revoke, which underrates coercion,
  material dependence and the coordination problem: nobody moves first.
- Later thinkers in this project treat that gap as the actual subject. Hegemony,
  misrecognition and disciplinary power are all attempts to explain what La Boétie only names.
</Objections>
