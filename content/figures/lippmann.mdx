Lippmann is the most useful source in this project because he is not a critic. He is an
insider describing the job, approvingly, in print, in 1922.

<Thesis source="Public Opinion, 1922">
The environment is too large and complex to know directly, so people act on stereotypes, the
pictures in their heads, and those pictures can be managed.
</Thesis>

## The argument

Nobody can experience national policy, a foreign war or an economy at first hand. What
reaches you is a report, a photograph, a phrase, and you fill in the rest from what you
already believe. Lippmann called those shortcuts stereotypes, and he meant it descriptively:
they are how anyone copes with a world larger than their senses.

He coined <Term id="manufacture-of-consent">the manufacture of consent</Term> for the work of
shaping those pictures. His conclusion is the part people forget: since the public cannot
govern competently on second-hand pictures, a specialized governing class should do the
governing and manage opinion on its behalf.

## Why that matters

[Herman and Chomsky](/domains/media) borrowed his phrase for a book arguing the opposite
politics. The phrase is the same, the evaluation is inverted, and the underlying description
of the mechanism is nearly identical. When the critic and the practitioner agree on how
something works and disagree only about whether it is good, the description is probably
right.

<Objections>
- The elitist conclusion does not follow from the epistemic premise. That people rely on
  simplifications is an argument for better information, not for a managing class.
- John Dewey's answer, made at the time, is that publics are formed by participation rather
  than by being informed at, and that Lippmann had described a symptom of exclusion as a fact
  about human capacity.
- The account of stereotypes is close to unfalsifiable in the form he gives it, and the
  experimental work on framing came much later.
</Objections>
