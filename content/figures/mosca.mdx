<Thesis source="The Ruling Class, 1896">
An organized minority always rules a disorganized majority, whatever the formal system is
called.
</Thesis>

## The argument

Organization beats numbers. A minority that can coordinate, communicate and act together
defeats a majority that cannot, and the majority's size is precisely what stops it
coordinating. This holds under monarchy, under democracy, and under any arrangement yet
tried.

The interesting half is what Mosca calls the
<Term id="political-formula">political formula</Term>: the story a ruling class tells to
explain why it should rule. Divine right. The will of the people. Expertise. Merit.

His insight is that the formula is believed sincerely, by the rulers first of all. It is not
a cynical cover story, and treating it as one gets the mechanism wrong. A ruling group that
did not believe its own formula would be far weaker, because the formula is what makes rule
feel legitimate to everyone including the ruled.

## Where it sits

Mosca is the elite-theory version of [Bourdieu's](/thinkers/bourdieu) ideology of giftedness
and [Gramsci's](/thinkers/gramsci) common sense: three descriptions of a legitimating story
that its beneficiaries believe.

C. Wright [Mills](/figures/mills) descends directly from this line, as does James Burnham.

<Objections>
- The claim is close to unfalsifiable. Any arrangement can be described as an organized
  minority ruling, which makes the theory good at describing and poor at predicting.
- It flattens the difference between ruling minorities that can be removed by an election and
  ones that cannot, and that difference matters to the people living under them.
- Politically the tradition ran toward accommodation with fascism, and the arguments were
  useful to it. That is a reason to read carefully rather than to skip.
</Objections>
