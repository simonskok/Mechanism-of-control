Fromm answers a question the rest of this site mostly leaves open: not how control is imposed,
but why so many people want it.

<Thesis source="Escape from Freedom, 1941">
Modern freedom cut people loose from the ties that told them who they were. It delivered
independence and, with it, isolation and powerlessness. Many will trade the first back to be
rid of the second.
</Thesis>

## The trade

Fromm wrote in exile, trying to explain how a modern, literate, industrial society had
produced mass enthusiasm for its own subjugation. His answer was that freedom has two
components that arrive together and are not experienced alike.

Freedom from external authority is gain. Freedom as being alone with your own choices, in a
society that has dissolved the guilds, the church, the fixed station and the settled village,
is a burden. He called the reaction the <Term id="fear-of-freedom">fear of freedom</Term>, and
identified three routes out of it: authoritarian submission, destructiveness, and automaton
conformity, where you simply become what is expected and stop noticing you have done it.

The third is the one he thought applied to democracies.

## Sadism as weakness

In *The Anatomy of Human Destructiveness* (1973) he sharpened the point that matters here.
Sadism, for Fromm, is the transformation of impotence into the experience of omnipotence. The
need to have absolute power over another person is what appears when the capacity to produce,
relate or create has failed.

That is the psychological statement of something this site argues structurally: control is
what gets installed where something else did not hold.

## Read alongside

[Adler](/figures/adler) has the individual version, written earlier.
[Paulo Freire](/counter-tradition) reaches the same fear of freedom from the other direction,
arguing that the oppressed resist liberation because it means giving up a known identity.
[Havel](/figures/havel) shows what the conformist route looks like when a whole society takes
it.

<Objections>
- The argument is interpretive rather than empirical. Fromm offers a reading of history and
  character, not evidence that could distinguish his account from a rival one.
- It risks explaining political outcomes by psychology, which underweights the material and
  institutional causes that a large literature treats as primary.
- The Authoritarian Personality research that grew from this milieu was heavily criticized on
  methodological grounds, and some of that criticism reaches back to the ideas underneath it.
- Automaton conformity is hard to test and easy to apply to anyone you disagree with.
</Objections>
