McClelland is the empirical support for a claim this site makes structurally elsewhere: that
the drive to control others and the capacity to govern yourself are different things, and that
the second is what decides what the first becomes.

<Thesis source="The Two Faces of Power, 1970">
Wanting power is not one motive. It is two, and what separates them is how much restraint sits
on top of the drive.
</Thesis>

## The two faces

McClelland split the need for power in two.

<Term id="personalized-power">Personalized power</Term> is influence wanted for its own sake
and treated as zero-sum, where your gain is someone else's loss. In his research it comes with
little inhibition or self-control, is exercised impulsively, and correlates with rudeness,
exploitation of subordinates, heavy drinking and decisions that damage the group.

<Term id="socialized-power">Socialized power</Term> is influence wanted in order to accomplish
something for a group. It does not involve less drive. It involves the same drive with
restraint on it.

## The variable that separates them

The distinguishing factor is what he called
<Term id="activity-inhibition">activity inhibition</Term>, a stable disposition to restrain
and regulate impulse. High power motive with high inhibition produces power used for
collective ends. High power motive with low inhibition produces the destructive version.

This matters for what a young reader should take from this site. It says that the problem is
not ambition or the wish to have an effect on the world, both of which are ordinary. The
problem is ambition without the self-government to shape it, and self-government is a
disposition rather than a fixed trait.

## Read alongside

[Alfred Adler](/figures/adler) made the same distinction sixty years earlier in clinical
rather than experimental language, separating striving over other people from striving toward
a task. [Tom Tyler](/figures/tyler) supplies the institutional version: authority that is
perceived as procedurally fair produces more compliance than authority that threatens.

<Objections>
- The measurement is contested. Motives were scored from stories people told about pictures, a
  method with known reliability problems, and results depend heavily on who is scoring.
- Activity inhibition is under-specified. McClelland never developed it at length, it was
  operationalized partly by counting negations in written responses, and researchers disagree
  on whether to include it in the leader motive profile at all.
- Much of the evidence is correlational and drawn from American managers, which limits what
  can be concluded about causation or about anywhere else.
- The framework was developed for and funded by organizations wanting better managers, which
  is worth putting the [four questions](/tools) to.
</Objections>
