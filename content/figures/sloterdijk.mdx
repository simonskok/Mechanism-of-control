Sloterdijk attacks the assumption this entire site is built on, which is why he is here rather
than tucked into a reading list.

<Thesis source="Critique of Cynical Reason, 1983">
The Enlightenment expected that showing people the illusion would break it. Instead we
produced people who see the illusion clearly, describe it accurately, and change nothing.
</Thesis>

## Enlightened false consciousness

Classical ideology critique assumes the target is ignorance. People do not know how the
arrangement works, so you show them, and the showing does the work.

Sloterdijk argues that this describes a world that no longer exists. The dominant modern
condition is <Term id="enlightened-false-consciousness">enlightened false consciousness</Term>:
a person who is well informed, unillusioned, entirely able to describe the mechanism they are
inside, and who participates anyway. He calls the resulting posture cynicism, and treats it
not as a failure of critique but as critique fully absorbed and rendered harmless.

The cynic is not a hypocrite. A hypocrite hides something. The cynic hides nothing, which is
what makes the position so difficult to argue with.

## Why this is the hardest objection on the site

If Sloterdijk is right, the theory of change underneath almost every project like this one is
wrong. Explaining a mechanism to someone who already half knows it does not produce action.
It produces a better-informed version of the same behaviour, plus a faint sense of having
done something.

The related idea, from Slavoj Zizek's reading of Sloterdijk, is that ideology lives in what
people do rather than in what they believe. The formula is that they know very well what they
are doing and they are doing it anyway. Zizek also supplies the category most useful here,
the <Term id="unknown-knowns">unknown knowns</Term>: the commitments you act on daily and have
never examined. Those, not missing facts, are what a site like this is actually up against.

## What survives

Sloterdijk's own answer is not more critique. He looks to a tradition he calls kynicism,
after Diogenes, where the response to a false order is not a better argument but a changed
practice, performed in public, that makes the order look absurd.

Whether or not that works, the structural point stands for anything built from this material.
A reader who agrees costs you nothing. The test is whether a page changes what someone does,
and agreement is not evidence of that.

<Objections>
- The book is a work of literary philosophy, not an empirical claim. There is no measurement
  of how widespread cynicism is, and the diagnosis of an entire era rests on interpretation.
- Kynicism as a remedy is thin. Diogenes in a barrel is an image, not a programme, and
  Sloterdijk has been criticized for offering a posture where an argument was needed.
- His later work on anthropotechnics and human self-breeding drew serious criticism, including
  a public dispute with Habermas, and some readers hold that it colours the earlier work.
- The claim may be overstated. Recent evidence that people generally do update their beliefs
  when corrected suggests the picture of the unmovable cynic is at least too flattering to
  itself.
</Objections>
