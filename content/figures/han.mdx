If any text in this project lands immediately with a reader under forty, it is this one.

<Thesis source="The Burnout Society, 2010, and Psychopolitics, 2014">
The disciplinary society produced madmen and criminals. The achievement society produces
depressives and burnouts.
</Thesis>

## The shift

[Foucault's](/thinkers/foucault) disciplinary subject lived under prohibition: you may not.
It produced the deviant, the delinquent, the patient.

Han's <Term id="achievement-society">achievement society</Term> runs on the positive: you
can. Nothing forbids you. The limit is now internal, so failure is a verdict on you rather
than on an obstacle, and there is nobody to resist because you are simultaneously the
employer and the employee, the guard and the prisoner.

Exploitation without a master is more efficient than exploitation with one, because it feels
like ambition. And it is more complete, because it does not stop when you leave the building.

## Where it sits

This is [Rose](/figures/rose) on the governable self written as a howl rather than a history,
and it is the present-tense end of [Deleuze's](/figures/deleuze) modulation: no graduation,
no discharge, and now no employer to blame either.

<Objections>
- Almost no empirical support. Han writes short philosophical essays, and the sweeping claims
  about depression and burnout are asserted rather than shown.
- The picture fits salaried knowledge workers in rich countries. Warehouse workers, care
  staff and delivery riders are disciplined by supervisors and algorithms in ways the
  achievement society barely describes.
- Framing burnout as self-exploitation risks doing the thing it criticizes, by relocating a
  structural problem inside the individual.
</Objections>
