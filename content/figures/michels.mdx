<Thesis source="Political Parties, 1911">
Who says organization says oligarchy.
</Thesis>

## Why the case matters more than the claim

Anyone can argue that organizations grow rulers. Michels went and looked, and he chose the
hardest possible case: the German Social Democratic Party, the largest and most internally
democratic mass party in Europe at the time, explicitly committed to equality and run by
people who meant it.

He found a permanent leadership stratum forming anyway, with interests distinct from the
members. Not through betrayal. Through the ordinary requirements of running anything at
scale: someone has to handle correspondence, someone becomes the person who knows the files,
expertise accumulates, the leadership acquires a stake in the organization's survival, and
survival starts to outrank the goals the organization was built for.

That is why the finding is called the
<Term id="iron-law-of-oligarchy">iron law of oligarchy</Term> rather than a tendency, and why
it is structural rather than moral.

## The unresolved conflict at the centre of this project

[Gramsci's](/thinkers/gramsci) answer to hegemony is counter-hegemonic organization: build
institutions, train organic intellectuals, win the culture over decades.

Michels says organization is where the problem starts.

Both cannot be fully right, and nobody has adequately worked the tension. It bears directly
on anything built out of this material, which is why it sits in the
[open questions](/open-questions).

<Objections>
- One case, however well chosen, is one case. The law is stated with a confidence a single
  party study cannot carry.
- Later research on unions and parties found real variation in how oligarchic organizations
  become, which suggests a tendency that conditions can strengthen or weaken rather than a
  law.
- Michels himself ended up supporting Italian fascism, which is at minimum a reason to ask
  what the argument was doing for him.
</Objections>
