<Thesis source="Necropolitics, 2003">
Biopolitics describes the metropole. From the colony the same power appears as the sovereign
right to decide who may be exposed to death.
</Thesis>

## The correction

[Foucault's](/thinkers/foucault) <Term id="biopolitics">biopolitics</Term> is a power that
makes live: it manages health, birth rates and hygiene, and lets die by neglect.

Mbembe's argument is that this reads modern power from inside Europe. In the colony the same
period produced a power organized around the administration of death: the plantation, the
occupation, the camp, the state of siege as a permanent condition rather than an emergency.
He calls it <Term id="necropolitics">necropolitics</Term>.

The claim is not only moral. It is chronological. Many of the techniques later described as
modern European government were developed at the periphery and reimported: identification
systems, pass laws, camps, aerial policing, emergency law.

## Why this project needs him

Every milieu on this site is North Atlantic. That is the largest gap in the material and it
is recorded as such in the [open questions](/open-questions). Mbembe, with
[Mitchell on Egypt](/milieus/infrastructural-power), is the entry point to the version of
this project that does not yet exist.

He is also the sharpest test of [Agamben](/thinkers/agamben): the camp as the paradigm of
modernity looks different when you note that the camp had a colonial history before it had a
European one.

<Objections>
- The essay generalizes across colonial situations that differed enormously, in the same way
  Agamben is criticized for generalizing the camp.
- Necropolitics has become a label applied to almost any state violence, which drains the
  precision that made it useful.
- As with much of this line, the analysis is far stronger on how domination works than on
  what would end it.
</Objections>
