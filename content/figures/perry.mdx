Perry studied exactly the readers this site is written for, and found that encountering
contested knowledge produces failure modes as often as it produces growth.

<Thesis source="Forms of Intellectual and Ethical Development in the College Years, 1970">
Undergraduates arrive believing that knowledge is right answers held by authorities. What
happens when that stops being true is not one thing.
</Thesis>

## The scheme

Perry interviewed Harvard and Radcliffe students across their years of study and mapped a
sequence.

They begin in dualism: there are right answers, authorities have them, the job is to receive
them. They move into multiplicity: on some questions the authorities disagree, so perhaps
everyone has a right to an opinion. Then into relativism: knowledge is contextual, claims are
argued for rather than announced, and even the authorities are arguing. Finally, and only
some of them, into commitment within relativism: choosing a position and living by it while
knowing it is not the only one available.

## The part that matters here

Perry did not describe a road everyone walks. He documented what happens to the people who do
not.

He named **retreat**, a return to dualism with fresh certainties and often with hostility
toward whatever produced the doubt. He named **temporizing**, where a student pauses at a
stage and stays there. And he named **escape**, a drift into detachment where relativism gets
used as a reason to be responsible for nothing, since if everything is a perspective then
nothing requires commitment.

Those are the two failure modes this site already worries about, found in undergraduates in
1970. Retreat is the reader who converts this material into a new hard certainty about who is
to blame. Escape is the reader who becomes cynical and does nothing.

## Why it changes the design

Commitment within relativism is the target, and Perry's finding is that it is not reached by
adding information. It is reached by having to act on a position while knowing it is
contestable. That is an argument for giving readers something to do rather than more to know.

<Objections>
- The sample was Harvard and Radcliffe students in the 1950s and 1960s, overwhelmingly male
  in the original interviews, and it is a considerable leap from there to a general account of
  intellectual development.
- Later researchers, including Belenky and colleagues in *Women's Ways of Knowing*, argued the
  scheme missed routes taken by people outside that population.
- Like all stage schemes it implies later is better, and the endpoint closely resembles the
  disposition of the liberal academy that produced it.
- The evidence is interview-based and interpreted by the researcher, with the usual risks that
  brings.
</Objections>
