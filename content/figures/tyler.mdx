Tyler supplies the evidence for something this site otherwise only asserts: that control built
on threat is weaker than the alternative, and that the alternative is measurable.

<Thesis source="Why People Obey the Law, 1990">
Compliance follows from legitimacy more reliably than from deterrence. People who judge the
procedure fair obey rules they disagree with.
</Thesis>

## Procedural justice

Tyler's research separates two reasons a person might comply with an authority. One is
instrumental: the likelihood of being caught and the size of the penalty. The other is
normative: whether the authority is regarded as entitled to be obeyed.

His consistent finding is that the second predicts compliance better than the first, and that
what drives it is <Term id="procedural-justice">procedural justice</Term>, meaning the fairness
of the process rather than the favourability of the outcome. People accept decisions that go
against them when they were treated with respect, given a voice, and dealt with by someone
whose neutrality and motives they could read.

Four elements recur: voice, neutrality, respectful treatment, and trustworthy motives.

## Why it belongs here

It is the empirical counterweight to a site that spends most of its length on how control
operates. It says the most durable form of order is the one that does not need much control,
and that this is not a moral preference but a finding about what produces compliance.

It also gives a concrete test for institutions a reader actually deals with. Were you given a
voice. Was the process the same for everyone. Were you treated as a person. Could you tell
what the decision-maker was trying to achieve. An institution failing those is relying on
threat, and by Tyler's evidence it is relying on the weaker instrument.

## The complication

Tyler's work has been adopted enthusiastically by police forces, and it is fair to ask what
happens when a legitimacy finding becomes a training programme. An institution can learn to
produce the appearance of voice, neutrality and respect without changing what it does. That is
the [Congress for Cultural Freedom](/milieus/congress-for-cultural-freedom) problem in a new
setting, and Tyler's own framework gives no way to tell the two apart from inside.

<Objections>
- Much of the evidence is correlational and based on self-report. People who already regard an
  authority as legitimate may simply report both fairness and compliance more favourably.
- Field experiments testing procedural justice training in policing have produced mixed
  results, and some have found no effect on the outcomes that matter.
- The framework can function as advice on how to secure compliance more cheaply, which is a
  use its critics point to, particularly where the underlying arrangement is unjust.
- The bulk of the research is American, and legitimacy is exactly the sort of thing that may
  not transfer across political cultures.
</Objections>
