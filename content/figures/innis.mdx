Innis was a political economist of staple goods, fur and timber and cod, before he turned to
communication. That is why his account of media is about weight, cost and transport rather
than about content.

<Thesis source="Empire and Communications, 1950, and The Bias of Communication, 1951">
Media biased toward time sustain tradition and religion. Media biased toward space sustain
administration and empire.
</Thesis>

## The bias

Stone, parchment and oral tradition are durable and hard to move. Societies built on them
hold continuity across generations and concentrate authority in priesthoods, because
knowledge lives in things that outlast people and in people who memorize.

Papyrus, print and the telegraph are light and fast. Societies built on them can administer
territory at distance, which is what an empire needs, and they trade permanence for reach.

Around whichever medium dominates, a
<Term id="monopoly-of-knowledge">monopoly of knowledge</Term> forms: the people who can work
it acquire authority that looks like competence and functions like power. A new medium breaks
that monopoly, and the group that formed around the old one loses without understanding why.

## Where it sits

Innis is sharper and far less quoted than McLuhan, who followed him at Toronto and made the
argument famous by making it slogan-shaped.

Read him next to [Mitchell on coal and oil](/milieus/infrastructural-power), which is the
same materialist move applied to energy rather than to writing.

<Objections>
- The time and space categories are suggestive rather than precise, and most real media sit
  awkwardly between them.
- The causal direction is asserted more often than demonstrated. Empires build roads and
  presses as well as being enabled by them.
- His late prose is compressed to the point of obscurity, which has let readers find in it
  whatever they brought.
</Objections>
