<MetaStrip />

Most of the literature on control stays at the level of ideas. This cluster asks what
the authority is physically made of.

## Mann's distinction

**Michael Mann, *The Sources of Social Power* (four volumes, 1986 to 2012)** separates
<Term id="despotic-power">despotic power</Term>, what elites can do without negotiating
with anyone, from
<Term id="infrastructural-power">infrastructural power</Term>, the capacity to actually
penetrate society and implement a decision.

Modern states are historically low on the first and unprecedented on the second. That is
the sharpest available correction to the assumption that visible authoritarianism is the
main thing to watch. A state that has to negotiate with everyone, and can then reach
every address, every account and every record, is a new kind of object.

## Tilly

**Charles Tilly, "War Making and State Making as Organized Crime" (1985)** and
*Coercion, Capital, and European States* (1990). States as protection rackets that
succeeded and then legitimated themselves retrospectively. This is the evidentiary
version of Benjamin on law-making violence in the [law domain](/domains/law).

## Mitchell, and the best single argument here

**Timothy Mitchell, *Carbon Democracy* (2011).** Coal is concentrated, moved by rail,
and requires dense labour at choke points, which gave workers the strike and helped make
mass democracy possible. Oil is pumped, piped and shipped, and needs very few workers,
which engineered that leverage away.

Democratic power as a function of infrastructure geometry. Also *Rule of Experts* (2002)
on Egypt, which is one of the few places in this project where the analysis is not North
Atlantic.

## Classification as a silent instrument

**Bruno Latour** and science and technology studies generally, on how facts and standards
get built and then become invisible infrastructure. Pair with **Geoffrey Bowker and
Susan Leigh Star, *Sorting Things Out* (1999)**, on classification systems as political
instruments that nobody experiences as political. It connects directly to Scott on
<Term id="legibility">legibility</Term> in the
[bureaucracy domain](/domains/bureaucracy).

<Contested status="Largely rejected">
**Karl Wittfogel, *Oriental Despotism* (1957)** and the hydraulic-state thesis, which
argued that societies dependent on large-scale irrigation produce despotic bureaucracies.
It has been largely rejected empirically and was shaped by Wittfogel's own break with
communism. It is included because it is historically important and because reading a
discredited argument carefully, for what it still gets right, is a skill worth having.
</Contested>
