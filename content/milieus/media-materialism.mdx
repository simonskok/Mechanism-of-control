Three distinct scenes making one claim: the medium's physical properties structure what
a society can think, and therefore who can rule it.

## Toronto, 1950s

**Harold Innis**, *Empire and Communications* (1950) and *The Bias of Communication*
(1951). Innis was a political economist of staple goods, fur and timber and cod, before
he turned to media, which is why his account is materialist rather than literary.

Media biased toward **time** (stone, parchment, oral tradition) are durable and hard to
move, and they sustain religion, tradition and continuity. Media biased toward **space**
(papyrus, print, telegraph) are light and fast, and they sustain administration, empire
and territorial control. A <Term id="monopoly-of-knowledge">monopoly of knowledge</Term>
forms around whoever masters the dominant medium, and a new medium breaks it.

Innis is sharper and much less quoted than McLuhan, who followed him at Toronto.

## Berlin, 1980s to 2000s

**Friedrich Kittler**, *Gramophone, Film, Typewriter* (1986). Media as hardware with
military ancestry, and the famous line that media determine our situation. Deliberately
anti-humanist, and useful precisely when a media argument has become too comfortable
about human agency.

## Paris

**Paul Virilio**, *Speed and Politics* (1977), and dromology: political power as the
control of speed and circulation, the state as an accelerator, and the accident as the
truth of the technology. **Vilém Flusser**, *Towards a Philosophy of Photography*
(1983): the apparatus programmes the user who believes they are using it.

## Older and larger

**Lewis Mumford**, *Technics and Civilization* (1934) and *The Myth of the Machine*
(1967 and 1970). The <Term id="megamachine">megamachine</Term>: the first machine in
history was made of human parts, organized by authority, and it built the pyramids
without a single piece of metal machinery. The distinction that matters to him is
authoritarian technics against democratic technics.

Mumford on the monastery clock is the concrete version of the timetable argument in
[education](/domains/education) and in [Foucault](/thinkers/foucault). The decisive
machine of the industrial age was not the steam engine. It was the clock, and it was
perfected by monks.

## One short essay to read now

Langdon Winner, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (1980). Whether technical arrangements
embody political orders, argued through concrete cases rather than assertion. Fifteen
pages.
