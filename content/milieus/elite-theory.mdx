## The climate

Mass suffrage was arriving and parties were becoming bureaucracies. These writers
watched the forms of democracy appear without the substance, and built a general theory
out of what they saw. Politically they run in several directions, some of them ugly, and
their arguments were later useful to fascism. Meet the arguments rather than avoiding
them.

## The three claims

**Gaetano Mosca, *The Ruling Class* (1896).** Every society is run by an organized
minority over a disorganized majority. Each ruling class legitimates itself with a
<Term id="political-formula">political formula</Term>: divine right, the will of the
people, expertise. The formula is believed sincerely by the rulers, which is what makes
it work and what makes cynicism the wrong lens for reading it.

**Vilfredo Pareto, *The Mind and Society* (1916).** Derivations are the reasons people
give. Residues are the drives underneath. History is the circulation of elites, one
group replacing another while the structure holds.

**Robert Michels, *Political Parties* (1911).** The
<Term id="iron-law-of-oligarchy">iron law of oligarchy</Term>. Organization itself
produces a permanent leadership stratum with interests distinct from the members.

Michels derived it from the German Social Democratic Party, the most internally
democratic mass party then in existence, which is what makes the finding devastating
rather than cynical. He was not describing a corrupt organization. He was describing the
best available one.

## What it adds

This is the standing objection to [Gramsci](/thinkers/gramsci). Gramsci's answer to
hegemony is counter-hegemonic organization. Michels says organization is where the
problem starts. Both cannot be fully right, nobody has adequately worked the tension,
and it bears directly on anything built out of this material. It sits in the
[open questions](/open-questions) for that reason.

C. Wright Mills descends directly from this line, as does James Burnham, *The Managerial
Revolution* (1941) and *The Machiavellians* (1943).

## Adjacent, and written under occupation

Bertrand de Jouvenel, *On Power* (1945), argues that centralizing power expands by
liberating individuals from intermediate bodies such as guilds, churches and local
authorities. Emancipation and centralization advance together, each one clearing the
ground for the other. It was written in occupied France and it reaches a pessimism
comparable to [Schmitt's](/thinkers/schmitt) by a completely different route.
